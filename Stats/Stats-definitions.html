M1 · Basic Probability & Statistics · 26 items

Types of data

Categorical (qualitative) data place each unit in a named category (eye colour, marital status); numerical (quantitative) data are numbers. Numerical data are discrete when they take countable values obtained by counting (defects per hour) and continuous when they can take any value in an interval, obtained by measuring (weight, time).

1.1.1 · slides 9, 11–12

Levels of measurement

Nominal: categories with no order (gender, party). Ordinal: ordered categories without meaningful differences (grades, satisfaction levels). Interval: ordered with meaningful differences but no true zero, so ratios are meaningless (°C, °F). Ratio: interval with a true zero, so ratios are meaningful (height, weight).

1.1.1 · slides 10–12

Arithmetic mean

The sum of the observations divided by their number: \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\) for a sample, \(\mu = \frac{\sum x_i}{N}\) for a population, and \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f x}{\sum f}\) for grouped data with frequencies \(f\).

1.1.2 · slides 14–17

Median

The value that divides the ordered data into two equal halves: the middle value when \(n\) is odd, and the average of the \(\frac{n}{2}\)-th and \(\left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right)\)-th values when \(n\) is even. It is the 50th percentile, \(Q_2\).

1.1.2 · slides 20–22, 822–823

Mode

The value or category with the highest frequency. A data set with one mode is unimodal, with two bimodal, with three trimodal and with more multimodal; if all values occur equally often there is no mode.

1.1.2 · slides 18–19, 824

Skewness

Asymmetry of a distribution. Positive (right) skew has a long right tail and mean > median > mode; negative (left) skew has a long left tail and mean < median < mode; a symmetric distribution has mean = median = mode.

1.1.3 · slide 23

Range

The difference between the largest and smallest observations, \(\text{Range} = x_{\max} - x_{\min}\); for continuous data the upper real limit of the maximum minus the lower real limit of the minimum is used.

1.1.4 · slide 29

Deviation and sum of squares

A deviation is the distance of an observation from the mean, \(x_i - \bar{x}\); deviations always sum to 0. The sum of squares is the total of the squared deviations, \(SS = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum x_i^2 - \frac{(\sum x_i)^2}{n}\).

1.1.4 · slides 31–33

Variance

The mean squared deviation from the mean. Population variance is \(\sigma^2 = \frac{SS}{N}\) (deviations from \(\mu\)); sample variance is \(s^2 = \frac{SS}{n - 1}\), whose \(n - 1\) divisor makes it an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

1.1.4 · slides 33–39

Standard deviation

The positive square root of the variance, \(\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}\) for a population and \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\) for a sample. It measures the typical distance of observations from the mean in the original units of the data.

1.1.4 · slides 30, 35, 39, 42

Quartiles and percentiles

The \(p\)-th percentile is the value below which \(p\%\) of the ordered data lie. The quartiles \(Q_1\), \(Q_2\), \(Q_3\) are the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles; the slides locate \(Q_k\) at position \(\frac{k(n+1)}{4}\), while the webinar takes the median of each half.

1.1.5 · slides 43–44, 834

Interquartile range and quartile deviation

The interquartile range is the spread of the middle 50% of the data, \(\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1\), and is not affected by extreme values. The quartile (semi-interquartile) deviation is half of it, \(QD = \frac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2}\).

1.1.4 · slide 44

Five-number summary

The five values minimum, \(Q_1\), median, \(Q_3\) and maximum, which together describe the centre, spread and shape of a data set and define a box plot.

1.1.5 · slides 43, 829, 835

Outlier and fences

An outlier is an observation unusually far from the bulk of the data. Points below the lower fence \(Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}\) or above the upper fence \(Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}\) are potential outliers; points beyond \(Q_1 - 3\,\text{IQR}\) or \(Q_3 + 3\,\text{IQR}\) are extreme (major) outliers.

1.1.5 · slides 45–48

Box-and-whisker plot

A graph of the five-number summary: a box from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\) with a line at the median, whiskers extending to the most extreme values within the fences, and outliers plotted individually.

1.1.5 · slides 45–46, 831–832

Random experiment and sample space

A random experiment is any action whose outcome is not known in advance, although the possible outcomes are (tossing a coin, drawing a card). Its sample space \(S\) is the set of all possible outcomes; for one die, \(S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\).

1.2.1 · slides 59–61, 92

Event

A subset of the sample space, that is, a set of outcomes. It occurs if the outcome of the experiment belongs to it; the empty set \(\varnothing\) is the impossible event and \(S\) the certain event.

1.2.1 · slide 62

Complement, union and intersection

The complement \(A^c\) (also \(A'\) or \(\bar{A}\)) is the set of outcomes not in \(A\), so \(P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)\). The union \(A \cup B\) is the event that \(A\) or \(B\) or both occur; the intersection \(A \cap B\) is the event that both occur. A Venn diagram pictures these as regions of a rectangle representing \(S\).

1.2.2 · slides 63–64

De Morgan's laws

The identities \((A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c\) ("neither \(A\) nor \(B\)") and \((A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c\) ("not both").

1.2.2 · slides 64, 71

Classical and empirical probability

Classical probability, for equally likely outcomes, is \(P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)}\), favourable outcomes over total outcomes. Empirical (relative-frequency) probability is the proportion of times \(A\) occurs in a large number of repetitions, in the limit as the number of trials grows.

1.2.3 · slides 66–67

Axioms of probability

Kolmogorov's axioms: \(P(A) \ge 0\) for every event \(A\); \(P(S) = 1\); and for mutually exclusive events \(A_1, A_2, \dots\), \(P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i P(A_i)\) (countable additivity).

1.2.3 · slide 68

Addition rule

For any two events, \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\); for mutually exclusive events it reduces to \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\).

1.2.4 · slide 70

Combination

The number of ways to choose \(r\) objects from \(n\) distinct objects without regard to order, \(\binom{n}{r} = {}^{n}C_{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n - r)!}\).

1.2.5 · slide 86

Mutually exclusive events

Events that cannot occur together, \(A \cap B = \varnothing\), so \(P(A \cap B) = 0\).

1.2.6 · slides 65, 73, 92

Independent events

Events for which the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\), equivalently \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\) when \(P(A) > 0\).

1.2.6 · slides 72–73, 109

Contingency table and marginal probability

A contingency table cross-classifies observations by two categorical variables; a cell divided by the grand total is a joint probability \(P(A_i \cap B_j)\). A marginal probability ignores the other variable, \(P(A_i) = \sum_j P(A_i \cap B_j)\), the row or column total written in the table's margin.

1.2.7, 3.1.7 · slides 91, 102, 254–255
M2 · Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem · 23 items

Conditional probability

The probability of \(B\) given that \(A\) has occurred, \(P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}\) for \(P(A) > 0\); \(A\) acts as a reduced sample space.

2.1.1 · slides 99–100

Multiplication and chain rules

The multiplication rule \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)\) rearranges the definition of conditional probability. Its extension to several events is the chain rule, \(P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\,P(C \mid A \cap B)\).

2.1.2 · slides 100–101, 136–137

Sampling with and without replacement

With replacement, each selected item is returned before the next draw, so draws are independent and probabilities stay constant. Without replacement, items are not returned, so each draw changes the composition of the pool and the draws are dependent.

2.1.4 · slides 112–113, 116

Partition and law of total probability

A partition is a set of events \(A_1, \dots, A_k\) that are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so exactly one occurs. For any event \(B\), the law of total probability (rule of elimination) states \(P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A_i)\,P(B \mid A_i)\).

2.1.5 · slides 118–121, 150

Tree diagram

A branching diagram whose first branches carry the probabilities of a partition and whose second branches carry conditional probabilities; the product along a path is a joint probability and the sum over paths is a total probability.

2.1.5 · slides 121, 853

Simpson's paradox

A reversal in which an association that holds within every subgroup disappears or reverses when the subgroups are combined, because the subgroups contribute different proportions to the totals.

2.1.5, 2.1.3 · slides 118–121

Bayes' theorem

For a partition \(E_1, \dots, E_n\) with \(P(E_i) > 0\) and an event \(A\) with \(P(A) > 0\), \(P(E_i \mid A) = \frac{P(E_i)\,P(A \mid E_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(E_j)\,P(A \mid E_j)}\); it gives the probability of a cause given an observed effect.

2.2.1 · slides 148–151, 865

Prior probability

The probability \(P(H)\) assigned to a hypothesis or cause before the current evidence is taken into account.

2.2.2 · slides 158, 866

Likelihood

The probability of the observed evidence under a given hypothesis, \(P(E \mid H)\), viewed as a measure of how well \(H\) explains \(E\).

2.2.2 · slides 158, 866

Evidence (normalising constant)

The total probability of the evidence over all hypotheses, \(P(E) = \sum_i P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)\); dividing by it makes the posteriors sum to 1.

2.2.2 · slide 158

Posterior probability

The updated probability of a hypothesis after observing evidence, \(P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}\); over mutually exclusive, exhaustive hypotheses \(\sum_i P(H_i \mid E) = 1\).

2.2.2 · slides 158, 866

False positive and false negative

A false positive is a positive test or classification when the condition is absent (a non-spam email flagged as spam); a false negative is a negative result when the condition is present. Their rates are \(P(+ \mid \text{absent})\) and \(P(- \mid \text{present})\).

2.2.3 · slides 156–157

Base-rate fallacy

The error of judging \(P(\text{condition} \mid \text{evidence})\) by \(P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{condition})\) while ignoring the prior (base rate) of the condition, which overstates the posterior when the condition is rare.

2.2.3 · slides 154–157

Likelihood ratio and posterior odds

The likelihood ratio is \(\frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)}\). Bayes' theorem in odds form states that posterior odds equal prior odds times the likelihood ratio, \(\frac{P(H_1 \mid E)}{P(H_2 \mid E)} = \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)} \cdot \frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)}\).

2.2.2, 2.2.3 · slide 158

Bayesian learning

Learning in which prior probabilities of hypotheses are combined with observed data through Bayes' theorem to obtain posterior probabilities, so that each new observation can raise or lower the probability of each hypothesis.

2.2.4 · slides 162, 167

MAP and ML hypotheses

The maximum a posteriori hypothesis is the most probable one given the data, \(h_{MAP} = \arg\max_{h} P(D \mid h)\,P(h)\). The maximum likelihood hypothesis ignores priors, \(h_{ML} = \arg\max_{h} P(D \mid h)\); the two coincide when all priors are equal.

2.2.4 · slide 163

Conditional independence

\(X\) is conditionally independent of \(Y\) given \(Z\) if \(P(X \mid Y, Z) = P(X \mid Z)\) for all values, equivalently \(P(X, Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z)\,P(Y \mid Z)\).

2.2.5 · slides 170–171

Naive Bayes assumption

The assumption that all features are conditionally independent given the class, \(P(X_1, \dots, X_n \mid Y) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid Y)\).

2.2.5 · slides 171, 881–882

Generative model

A model that estimates the class-conditional likelihood \(P(X \mid Y)\) and the prior \(P(Y)\), and obtains the posterior \(P(Y \mid X)\) by Bayes' theorem, rather than modelling \(P(Y \mid X)\) directly.

2.2.6 · slide 169

Naive Bayes classifier

A classifier that assigns an instance to \(\hat{Y} = \arg\max_{y_k} P(Y = y_k)\prod_i P(X_i = x_i \mid Y = y_k)\). The probabilities are estimated by relative frequencies: a frequency table counts each feature value with each class, and dividing by the class totals gives the likelihood table.

2.2.6 · slides 165–168, 172–174, 880, 886

Zero-frequency problem

The situation in which a feature value never occurs with a class in the training data, so its estimated likelihood is 0 and the whole product \(P(y)\prod_i P(x_i \mid y)\) becomes 0 regardless of the other features.

2.2.7 · slides 177, 885

Laplace (add-one) smoothing

A correction that adds 1 to every count, \(P(x_i = v \mid y) = \frac{\text{count}(v, y) + 1}{\text{count}(y) + k}\), where \(k\) is the number of possible values of the feature (the vocabulary size for words), so that no estimate is 0.

2.2.7 · slides 178, 187–190

Gaussian, multinomial and Bernoulli Naive Bayes

Gaussian NB models each continuous feature within class \(y\) as normal, \(P(x_i \mid y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_y^2}}e^{-(x_i - \mu_y)^2/(2\sigma_y^2)}\). Multinomial NB models feature counts (word frequencies) and Bernoulli NB binary features (word present or absent), both standard for text.

2.2.8 · slides 185, 883
M3a · Random Variables & Joint Distributions · 13 items

Random variable

A function \(X: S \to \mathbb{R}\) that assigns exactly one real number to each outcome of a random experiment.

3.1.1 · slides 220, 894

Discrete and continuous random variables

A discrete random variable takes a finite or countably infinite set of values (number of heads). A continuous random variable takes any value in an interval of the real line (a person's height, a waiting time).

3.1.1 · slides 221–222, 895

Probability mass function (pmf)

For a discrete random variable, the function \(p(x) = P(X = x)\), which must satisfy \(p(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x\) and \(\sum_x p(x) = 1\).

3.1.2 · slides 223–224

Discrete uniform distribution

The distribution of a discrete random variable with \(n\) equally likely values, \(p(x) = \frac{1}{n}\); for values \(1, \dots, n\) its mean is \(\frac{n+1}{2}\) and variance \(\frac{n^2 - 1}{12}\).

3.1.2 · slide 225

Expected value

The probability-weighted average \(E[X] = \mu = \sum_x x\,p(x)\) (or \(\int x f(x)\,dx\)), with \(E[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p(x)\). A game is fair when the expected net gain \(E[X]\) is zero.

3.1.3 · slides 227–228, 231

Variance of a random variable

The expected squared deviation from the mean, \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2\); its square root \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, and \(\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)\).

3.1.4 · slides 229–231

Cumulative distribution function (CDF)

The function \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\), non-decreasing from 0 to 1. For a discrete variable it is a step function, \(F(x) = \sum_{t \le x} p(t)\); for a continuous one \(F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt\) and \(F'(x) = f(x)\).

3.1.5 · slides 232–233

Probability density function (pdf)

For a continuous random variable, a function with \(f(x) \ge 0\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\) such that \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\); consequently \(P(X = a) = 0\).

3.1.6 · slides 240–243

Exponential distribution

The continuous distribution with pdf \(f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x \ge 0\), CDF \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\), mean \(\frac{1}{\lambda}\) and variance \(\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\); it is memoryless, \(P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)\).

3.1.6 · slides 240–243

Joint probability distribution

For two random variables, the joint pmf \(p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)\) (discrete) or joint pdf \(f(x, y)\) with \(P((X, Y) \in A) = \iint_A f(x, y)\,dx\,dy\) (continuous); either must be non-negative and sum or integrate to 1. It is also called the bivariate distribution.

3.1.7 · slides 251–254, 908–909

Marginal distribution

The distribution of one variable obtained from a joint distribution by summing or integrating out the other: \(p_X(x) = \sum_y p(x, y)\) or \(f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y)\,dy\).

3.1.7 · slides 255–256, 910

Conditional distribution

The distribution of \(Y\) given \(X = x\), \(p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p_X(x)}\) or \(f(y \mid x) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_X(x)}\) for \(f_X(x) > 0\).

3.1.7 · slides 258–259, 912–913

Independent random variables

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if their joint distribution factors into the marginals for all \((x, y)\): \(f(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\) (or \(p(x, y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)\)).

3.1.7 · slides 257, 911
M3b · Standard Distributions, Sampling, CLT & Estimation · 33 items

Bernoulli distribution

The distribution of a single trial with two outcomes, \(P(X = 1) = p\) and \(P(X = 0) = q = 1 - p\), that is \(p(x) = p^x q^{1-x}\) for \(x \in \{0, 1\}\); mean \(p\), variance \(pq\).

3.2.1 · slides 278–281

Binomial distribution

The distribution of the number of successes \(X\) in \(n\) independent trials, each with two outcomes and the same success probability \(p\): \(P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x}\), \(x = 0, \dots, n\); mean \(np\), variance \(npq\). Written \(X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)\).

3.2.2 · slides 283–284, 292

Poisson distribution

The distribution of the number of events in a fixed interval when events occur independently at a constant mean rate: \(P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}\), \(x = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), with mean = variance = \(\lambda\). For a rate per unit, \(\lambda = \text{rate} \times \text{interval length}\).

3.2.3 · slides 293, 296

Poisson approximation to the binomial

The use of \(\text{Poisson}(\lambda = np)\) in place of \(\text{Bin}(n, p)\) when \(n\) is large and \(p\) is small; the slides require \(n \ge 100\) and \(np \le 10\).

3.2.3 · slide 297

Normal distribution

The continuous distribution with pdf \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\), \(-\infty < x < \infty\), written \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\); it is bell-shaped and symmetric about \(\mu\), with mean = median = mode = \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\).

3.2.4 · slides 303–304, 307

Standard normal distribution and z-score

The standard normal is \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\), whose CDF \(\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z)\) is tabulated. The z-score \(z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\) counts standard deviations from the mean; if \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) then \(\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0, 1)\).

3.2.4 · slides 308–310

Empirical (68–95–99.7) rule

For a normal distribution, about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean, \(\mu \pm \sigma\), \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\), \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\).

3.2.4 · slides 305–306

Normal approximation to the binomial

The use of \(N(\mu = np, \sigma^2 = npq)\) for a binomial count when \(n\) is large; the course rule requires \(np \ge 15\) and \(nq \ge 15\) (texts also use 10 or 5).

3.2.5 · slide 317

Continuity correction

The adjustment of 0.5 made to a discrete boundary when a discrete distribution is approximated by a continuous one, for example \(P(X \le 13) \approx P(Y < 13.5)\) and \(P(X \ge 14) \approx P(Y > 13.5)\).

3.2.5 · slide 318

Degrees of freedom

The number of independent values free to vary in computing a statistic, usually the sample size minus the number of parameters estimated (for \(s^2\), \(n - 1\)); it indexes the \(\chi^2\), \(t\) and \(F\) distributions.

3.2.6, 1.1.4 · slides 322, 39

Chi-square distribution

The distribution of \(\chi^2_k = Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_k^2\) for independent \(Z_i \sim N(0, 1)\), with \(k\) degrees of freedom; it is right-skewed on \([0, \infty)\) with mean \(k\) and variance \(2k\).

3.2.6 · slides 322–323

Student's t distribution

The distribution of \(t_k = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{\chi^2_k / k}}\) for independent \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\) and \(\chi^2_k\); symmetric and bell-shaped with heavier tails than \(N(0, 1)\), it tends to \(N(0, 1)\) as \(k \to \infty\).

3.2.6 · slides 324–326

F distribution

The distribution of \(F(\nu_1, \nu_2) = \frac{\chi^2_{\nu_1}/\nu_1}{\chi^2_{\nu_2}/\nu_2}\) for independent chi-squares, with numerator df \(\nu_1\) and denominator df \(\nu_2\); right-skewed on \([0, \infty)\), with \(F_{1-\alpha}(\nu_1, \nu_2) = \frac{1}{F_{\alpha}(\nu_2, \nu_1)}\).

3.2.6 · slides 327–328

Population, census and sample

The population is the complete set of \(N\) elements of interest, and a census collects data on all of them. A sample is a subset of \(n\) elements selected from the population, from which conclusions about it are drawn.

3.3.1 · slides 349, 411

Parameter and statistic

A parameter is a fixed, usually unknown population characteristic (\(\mu\), \(\sigma^2\), \(P\), \(\rho\)). A statistic is computed from sample data (\(\bar{x}\), \(s^2\), \(\hat{p}\), \(r\)), varies from sample to sample, and is used to estimate a parameter.

3.3.1 · slides 351, 390

Inferential statistics

The use of sample data to draw conclusions about a population, through point estimation, interval estimation and hypothesis testing.

3.3.1 · slides 350, 388

Simple random sampling (SRS)

Selection of \(n\) units from a finite population so that every unit, and every possible sample of size \(n\), has the same chance of being chosen.

3.3.2 · slides 357–358

Systematic random sampling

Selection from an ordered sampling frame of a random start among the first \(k\) units and then every \(k\)-th unit, where \(k = \frac{N}{n}\) is the sampling interval.

3.3.2 · slide 358

Stratified random sampling

Division of a heterogeneous population into non-overlapping, internally homogeneous strata, followed by a simple random sample from each stratum, usually in proportion to stratum size (proportional allocation \(n_h = n \frac{N_h}{N}\)).

3.3.2 · slide 359

Multistage sampling

Sampling in stages: first select larger units (clusters such as districts or schools) at random, then select smaller units within the chosen clusters.

3.3.2 · slide 357

Non-probability sampling

Selection without a known probability structure: judgement (purposive) sampling picks units the researcher thinks representative, convenience sampling picks easy-to-reach units, quota sampling fills fixed numbers per category non-randomly, and snowball sampling recruits through referrals from earlier participants.

3.3.2 · slide 357

Bias (in sampling)

A systematic error that makes estimates consistently differ from the true value in one direction, arising from the sampling method or measurement; in the model observed data = truth + bias + random error, it does not shrink as \(n\) increases.

3.3.2 · slide 356

Sampling error and sampling variation

Sampling error is the difference between a statistic and the parameter it estimates, arising because only a sample is observed. Sampling variation is the change in a statistic's value from one random sample to another, which makes it a random variable; both shrink as \(n\) increases.

3.3.3 · slides 360–365

Sampling distribution

The probability distribution of a statistic (such as \(\bar{x}\) or \(\hat{p}\)) over all possible samples of a given size from a population; it depends on the population, the sample size and the sampling method.

3.3.4 · slides 366, 370

Standard error

The standard deviation of a statistic's sampling distribution; for the sample mean \(SE(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) and for a sample proportion \(SE(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{PQ}{n}}\).

3.3.4, 3.3.6 · slides 375, 386

Finite population correction

The factor \(\sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}}\) by which the standard error is multiplied when sampling without replacement from a finite population of size \(N\); it is used when \(\frac{n}{N} \ge 0.05\) and tends to 1 as \(N\) grows.

3.3.4 · slides 380–381, 437

Central Limit Theorem

For a random sample of size \(n\) from any population with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\), the distribution of \(\bar{x}\) approaches \(N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\) as \(n\) grows (in practice \(n \ge 30\)), so \(Z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\) is approximately standard normal.

3.3.5 · slides 374–375

Sample proportion

The fraction of sample units having a characteristic, \(\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}\); its sampling distribution has mean \(P\) and standard error \(\sqrt{\frac{PQ}{n}}\), and is approximately normal when \(nP > 15\) and \(nQ > 15\).

3.3.6 · slides 383–386

Point estimate and estimator

An estimator is a rule (a statistic) for estimating a parameter, such as \(\bar{x}\) for \(\mu\); a point estimate is the single number it gives for a particular sample.

3.3.7 · slides 389–390, 485

Unbiased and consistent estimators

An estimator \(\hat{\theta}\) is unbiased if \(E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta\) (\(\bar{x}\) and \(s^2\) are; \(\frac{SS}{n}\) is not), and consistent if it converges in probability to \(\theta\) as the sample size grows, \(\hat{\theta}_n \to \theta\) as \(n \to \infty\).

3.3.7 · slide 485

Efficient and sufficient estimators

An efficient estimator has the smallest variance among unbiased estimators of the parameter. A sufficient estimator (statistic) contains all the information in the sample about the parameter, so no other function of the data adds to it; \(\sum x_i\) is sufficient for the Poisson \(\lambda\).

3.3.7 · slide 485

Confidence interval and confidence level

A confidence interval is estimate \(\pm\) critical value \(\times\) standard error, such as \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\). Its confidence level \(1 - \alpha\) (90%, 95%, 99%) is the proportion of such intervals, over repeated samples, that contain the parameter; it describes the method, not one interval.

3.3.8 · slides 391–395, 930–933

Margin of error

The half-width of a confidence interval, \(E = z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) for a mean; solving for \(n\) gives the required sample size \(n = \left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{E}\right)^2\), rounded up.

3.3.8 · slides 400, 939
M4 · Hypothesis Testing, MLE & ANOVA · 30 items

Null hypothesis

A statistical hypothesis is a claim about population parameters or a distribution's form. The null hypothesis \(H_0\) is the hypothesis of no difference or no effect, attributing any observed discrepancy to sampling variation; it always contains the equality (\(=\), \(\le\) or \(\ge\)).

4.1 · slides 413, 415, 940

Alternative hypothesis

The hypothesis \(H_1\) (or \(H_a\)) of a difference or effect, the claim the test seeks evidence for; it uses \(\ne\), \(<\) or \(>\) and determines the tail of the test.

4.1 · slides 413, 416

One-tailed and two-tailed tests

A left-tailed test (\(H_1: \theta < \theta_0\)) or right-tailed test (\(H_1: \theta > \theta_0\)) places the whole rejection region in one tail; a two-tailed test (\(H_1: \theta \ne \theta_0\)) splits it, \(\frac{\alpha}{2}\) in each tail.

4.1 · slides 421–423

Type I and Type II errors

A Type I error rejects \(H_0\) when it is true, with probability \(\alpha = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true})\). A Type II error fails to reject \(H_0\) when it is false, with probability \(\beta = P(\text{do not reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ true})\).

4.1 · slides 420, 426

Level of significance

The maximum probability of a Type I error that the tester is willing to accept, \(\alpha\), fixed before the test (commonly 0.05 or 0.01).

4.1 · slides 420, 426

Power of a test

The probability of correctly rejecting a false \(H_0\), \(1 - \beta\); it increases with sample size, effect size and \(\alpha\).

4.1 · slide 426

Test statistic

A statistic computed from the sample whose distribution under \(H_0\) is known, usually of the form \(\frac{\text{estimate} - \text{hypothesised value}}{\text{standard error}}\), such as \(z\), \(t\), \(\chi^2\) or \(F\).

4.1 · slides 425, 942

Critical region and critical value

The critical (rejection) region is the set of test-statistic values for which \(H_0\) is rejected, with total probability \(\alpha\) under \(H_0\); the critical value is its boundary, such as \(z_{0.025} = 1.96\), \(z_{0.05} = 1.645\), \(z_{0.005} = 2.576\), \(z_{0.01} = 2.326\).

4.1 · slides 421–423, 943

p-value

The probability, assuming \(H_0\) is true, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed (doubled tail area for a two-tailed test); \(H_0\) is rejected when \(p \le \alpha\).

4.1 · slides 420, 424, 944–945

Parametric and non-parametric tests

A parametric test assumes the data follow a distribution characterised by parameters, such as the normal (z-, t-, F-tests, ANOVA); a non-parametric test makes no such distributional assumption.

4.1 · slides 418–419

z-test

A test whose statistic is standard normal under \(H_0\), used for means when \(\sigma\) is known or \(n \ge 30\) and for proportions with large samples, for example \(z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\).

4.2 · slides 419, 425, 947

Pooled sample proportion

Under \(H_0: P_1 = P_2\), the combined estimate of the common proportion, \(\hat{p} = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}\), used in the standard error \(\sqrt{\hat{p}\hat{q}\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}\).

4.2 · slides 439–441, 973

One-sample t-test

A test of \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) for a small sample from a normal population with \(\sigma\) unknown, using \(t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\) with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom.

4.3 · slides 442–447

Unpaired (pooled) two-sample t-test

A test of \(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\) for two independent small samples from normal populations with equal variances, using the pooled variance \(s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\) and \(t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}}\) with \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\) df.

4.3 · slides 448–449, 948

Paired t-test

A test for two related measurements on the same subjects (before and after), applying the one-sample t-test to the differences: \(t = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d/\sqrt{n}}\) with \(n - 1\) df, testing \(H_0: \mu_d = 0\).

4.3 · slides 450–452

Chi-square test of independence

A test of whether two categorical variables in an \(r \times c\) contingency table are associated, using \(\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}\) with \((r - 1)(c - 1)\) degrees of freedom; \(H_0\) states the variables are independent.

4.4 · slides 453–457

Expected frequency

The count expected in a cell if \(H_0\) were true: in a contingency table \(E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{row } i \text{ total})(\text{column } j \text{ total})}{n}\); in a goodness-of-fit test \(E_i = n p_i\). Each should be at least 5.

4.4 · slides 455, 459, 464

Chi-square goodness-of-fit test

Karl Pearson's test of whether observed frequencies in \(k\) categories agree with those expected under a hypothesised distribution, using \(\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\) with \(k - 1 - m\) df, where \(m\) is the number of parameters estimated from the data.

4.4 · slides 467–471

Likelihood and log-likelihood

The likelihood is the joint pmf or pdf of the observed data viewed as a function of the parameter, \(L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i \mid \theta)\) for i.i.d. data. The log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = \sum_i \ln f(x_i \mid \theta)\) has the same maximiser and turns the product into a sum.

4.5 · slides 491, 494–495

Maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)

The parameter value \(\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_{\theta} L(\theta)\) that makes the observed data most probable, usually found by solving \(\frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = 0\); for example \(\hat{p} = \frac{k}{n}\), \(\hat{\lambda} = \bar{x}\), \(\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}\), \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{SS}{n}\).

4.5 · slides 484, 486, 495

Invariance property of the MLE

If \(\hat{\theta}\) is the MLE of \(\theta\), then \(g(\hat{\theta})\) is the MLE of \(g(\theta)\) for any function \(g\); for example the MLE of \(P(X = 0) = e^{-\lambda}\) is \(e^{-\bar{x}}\).

4.5 · slide 529

F-test for two variances

A test of \(H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\) for two independent normal samples, using \(F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}\) with the larger sample variance in the numerator and \((n_1 - 1, n_2 - 1)\) degrees of freedom.

4.6 · slides 501–503

Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

A method for testing the equality of three or more population means, \(H_0: \mu_1 = \dots = \mu_k\) against \(H_1\): not all \(\mu_j\) are equal, by comparing variation between groups with variation within groups; it assumes normality, equal variances and independent observations.

4.7 · slides 504–505

Treatment and factor

A factor is a categorical variable whose effect on the response is studied; its levels or groups are the treatments, and \(x_{ij}\) denotes observation \(i\) under treatment \(j\).

4.7 · slides 506, 517

ANOVA sums of squares

\(SST = \sum_j \sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar{x})^2\) is the total variation; \(SSTR = \sum_j n_j(\bar{x}_j - \bar{x})^2\) is the between-treatments variation; \(SSE = \sum_j \sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)^2\) is the within-treatments (error) variation; and \(SST = SSTR + SSE\).

4.7 · slides 508–509, 511

Mean squares, F-ratio and ANOVA table

Mean squares are sums of squares over their df, \(MSTR = \frac{SSTR}{k - 1}\) and \(MSE = \frac{SSE}{n - k}\), and \(F = \frac{MSTR}{MSE} \sim F(k - 1, n - k)\) under \(H_0\). The ANOVA table lists, for each source of variation, its SS, df, mean square and F-ratio.

4.7 · slides 507–508, 512, 521

Correction factor

The term \(CF = \frac{G^2}{n}\), where \(G\) is the grand total of all \(n\) observations, used in the shortcut formulas \(SST = \sum x_{ij}^2 - CF\) and \(SSTR = \sum_j \frac{C_j^2}{n_j} - CF\) (\(C_j\) the treatment totals).

4.7 · slides 514, 520

Two-way ANOVA, blocks and interaction

Two-way ANOVA studies a treatment factor while controlling a block factor, removing its variation from the error: \(SST = SSTR + SSBL + SSE\), with \((k - 1)(m - 1)\) error df without replication and F-ratios \(\frac{MSTR}{MSE}\), \(\frac{MSBL}{MSE}\). An interaction, where one factor's effect depends on the other's level, can be tested only with replication (several observations per cell).

4.7 · slides 517–521, 525–528

Post-hoc test and LSD

A post-hoc test compares pairs of means after ANOVA rejects \(H_0\). Fisher's least significant difference is \(LSD = t_{\alpha/2,\, n - k}\sqrt{MSE\left(\frac{1}{n_i} + \frac{1}{n_j}\right)}\); means \(i\) and \(j\) differ if \(\lvert \bar{x}_i - \bar{x}_j \rvert > LSD\).

4.7 · slides 504, 518

Multiple-testing problem and Bonferroni correction

Running many tests inflates the chance of at least one Type I error (to \(1 - (1 - \alpha)^m\) for \(m\) independent tests); the Bonferroni correction tests each at level \(\frac{\alpha}{m}\).

4.1, 4.7 · slides 420, 504
M5 · Prediction & Forecasting I — Correlation & Regression · 15 items

Covariance

A measure of the direction of the linear relationship between two variables: for a sample \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{n - 1}\), and for random variables \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]\). Its value depends on the units of measurement.

5.1 · slides 541–543, 547

Variance–covariance matrix

For variables \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), the symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix \(\Sigma = [\sigma_{ij}]\) with \(\sigma_{ii} = \operatorname{Var}(X_i)\) on the diagonal and \(\sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)\) off the diagonal.

5.1 · slide 544

Principal component analysis (PCA)

A dimension-reduction method that diagonalises the covariance matrix: the principal components are its eigenvectors, which give uncorrelated linear combinations of the variables, and each eigenvalue is the variance along its component.

5.1 · slides 550–551

Pearson's correlation coefficient

Correlation is the degree and direction of association between two variables. Karl Pearson's coefficient is the standardised covariance \(r = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{s_X s_Y} = \frac{n\sum XY - \sum X\sum Y}{\sqrt{n\sum X^2 - (\sum X)^2}\sqrt{n\sum Y^2 - (\sum Y)^2}}\), unit-free with \(-1 \le r \le 1\), measuring linear association (population value \(\rho\)).

5.2 · slides 552–554, 560, 978, 982–983

Scatter diagram

A plot of the \(n\) pairs \((x_i, y_i)\) as points, with the independent variable on the horizontal axis, used to judge the form, direction and strength of a relationship and to spot outliers.

5.2 · slides 563–573, 980–981

Spearman's rank correlation coefficient

The correlation between the ranks of two variables, \(R = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}\), where \(d_i\) is the difference between the ranks of the \(i\)-th pair; it measures monotone association and lies in \([-1, 1]\). With tied ranks (each tie given the average rank), \(\sum \frac{m(m^2 - 1)}{12}\) over tie groups of size \(m\) is added to \(\sum d_i^2\).

5.3 · slides 989–992

Confounding (lurking) variable

A variable not included in the analysis that influences both variables being studied, producing a correlation between them without a causal link.

5.2 · slides 553–555

Simple linear regression model

The model \(Y = a + bX + \varepsilon\) relating a dependent (response) variable \(Y\) to an independent (explanatory) variable \(X\), with intercept \(a\), slope \(b\) and random error \(\varepsilon\) assumed to have mean 0, constant variance \(\sigma^2\), independence and a normal distribution.

5.4 · slides 575–577

Least squares and normal equations

Least squares chooses \(a\) and \(b\) to minimise \(\sum (y_i - a - bx_i)^2\). Setting the partial derivatives to zero gives the normal equations \(\sum y = na + b\sum x\) and \(\sum xy = a\sum x + b\sum x^2\), with solution \(b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\), \(a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}\).

5.4, 5.6 · slides 578–580, 1007, 1011

Residual

The difference between an observed and a fitted value, \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\); least-squares residuals sum to 0.

5.4, 5.5 · slides 579, 588

Regression coefficients

The slopes of the two regression lines: \(b_{yx} = r\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x}\) for \(Y\) on \(X\), \(y - \bar{y} = b_{yx}(x - \bar{x})\), and \(b_{xy} = r\frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y}\) for \(X\) on \(Y\), \(x - \bar{x} = b_{xy}(y - \bar{y})\); \(r\) is their geometric mean, \(r^2 = b_{yx}b_{xy}\).

5.4 · slides 995–996, 998

Regression sums of squares

\(SST = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\) (total), \(SSR = \sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2\) (explained by regression) and \(SSE = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\) (error), related by \(SST = SSR + SSE\).

5.5 · slides 586–589

Coefficient of determination

The proportion of the total variation in \(Y\) explained by the regression, \(r^2 = \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}\), with \(0 \le r^2 \le 1\); in simple linear regression it is the square of Pearson's \(r\).

5.5 · slides 589–590

Extrapolation

Using a fitted regression to predict \(Y\) at values of \(X\) outside the range of the data used to fit it, which assumes the relationship continues unchanged where it was not observed.

5.4 · slides 584, 1001

Curve fitting

Fitting a non-linear form by least squares: a parabola \(y = a + bx + cx^2\) through three normal equations, or a power curve \(y = ax^b\) or exponential curve \(y = ae^{bx}\) (or \(y = ab^x\)) after taking logarithms to make the model linear in its parameters.

5.6 · slides 1011–1020
M6 · Prediction & Forecasting II — Time Series; GMM & EM · 35 items

Time series

A set of observations \(Y_1, Y_2, \dots, Y_n\) of one variable recorded sequentially at successive, equally spaced points in time, \(Y_t\) being the value at time \(t\); cross-sectional data, by contrast, record many units at one point in time.

6.1 · slides 603–605, 1023

Components of a time series

The trend \(T_t\) is the long-term rise or fall in level; the seasonal component \(S_t\) repeats over a fixed, known period of a year or less; the cyclical component \(C_t\) is a recurring but non-periodic swing usually longer than a year (business cycles); the irregular component \(I_t\) is the random residual left after the others are removed.

6.1 · slides 606–616

Additive and multiplicative models

The additive model \(Y_t = T_t + S_t + C_t + I_t\) assumes seasonal variation of constant size; the multiplicative model \(Y_t = T_t \times S_t \times C_t \times I_t\) assumes variation proportional to the level, and becomes additive after taking logarithms.

6.1 · slides 619–621

Forecasting, planning and horizons

Forecasting predicts future values of a series as accurately as possible from past data and known future events; goals are what one wants to happen, and planning is the action taken to reach them. Forecasts are short-term (scheduling), medium-term (budgets, resources) or long-term (strategy).

6.1 · slides 622–628

Qualitative and quantitative forecasting

Qualitative methods rely on judgement: personal opinion, panel consensus, the Delphi method (repeated anonymous expert rounds converging to consensus) and market research. Quantitative methods use data, either time-series methods (smoothing, trend projection) or causal methods (regression on explanatory variables).

6.1 · slide 629

Moving average

The \(k\)-period moving average is the mean of \(k\) consecutive values, \(MA_t = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1} Y_{t-i}\), recomputed as the window moves forward; it smooths the series and, as a forecast, \(F_{t+1} = MA_t\).

6.2 · slides 630, 635

Centred moving average

A moving average positioned at the centre of its window; for even \(k\) it is the average of two successive \(k\)-period averages (a \(2 \times k\) MA), so that it aligns with an actual time point.

6.2 · slides 631, 639

Weighted moving average

A moving average with weights \(w_i\) summing to 1, usually larger for recent values, \(F_{t+1} = \sum_i w_i Y_{t-i}\) (for example 0.5, 0.3, 0.2).

6.2 · slide 640

Least-squares trend line

A straight-line trend \(Y = a + bX\) fitted to a series by least squares, with time coded as \(X\) so that \(\sum X = 0\), giving \(a = \frac{\sum Y}{n}\) and \(b = \frac{\sum XY}{\sum X^2}\).

6.2 · slides 1034–1038

Forecast error, MSE and MAD

The forecast error is \(e_t = Y_t - F_t\). Mean squared error \(MSE = \frac{1}{n}\sum e_t^2\) weights large errors heavily; mean absolute deviation \(MAD = \frac{1}{n}\sum \lvert e_t \rvert\) weights them linearly, in the data's units.

6.3 · slides 632–633, 701–702

MAPE and LAD

Mean absolute percentage error \(MAPE = \frac{100\%}{n}\sum \frac{\lvert e_t \rvert}{Y_t}\), a scale-free measure undefined when some \(Y_t = 0\); largest absolute deviation \(LAD = \max_t \lvert e_t \rvert\), the worst single error.

6.3 · slides 633, 702

Simple exponential smoothing

The method \(F_{t+1} = \alpha Y_t + (1 - \alpha)F_t\), started with \(F_2 = Y_1\), a weighted average of all past values with weights \(\alpha(1 - \alpha)^j\) decaying with age \(j\). The smoothing constant \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) weights the newest value: \(\alpha = 1\) gives the naive forecast \(F_{t+1} = Y_t\), \(\alpha = 0\) never updates.

6.4 · slides 696–697, 703, 716

Holt's double exponential smoothing

Exponential smoothing with a level and a trend: \(L_t = \alpha Y_t + (1 - \alpha)(L_{t-1} + T_{t-1})\), \(T_t = \beta(L_t - L_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta)T_{t-1}\), and forecast \(F_{t+k} = L_t + kT_t\).

6.4 · slides 717–719

Holt–Winters triple exponential smoothing

Holt's method plus a multiplicative seasonal index \(S\) of period \(M\): \(L_t = \alpha\frac{Y_t}{S_{t-M}} + (1 - \alpha)(L_{t-1} + T_{t-1})\), \(T_t = \beta(L_t - L_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta)T_{t-1}\), \(S_t = \gamma\frac{Y_t}{L_t} + (1 - \gamma)S_{t-M}\), \(F_{t+1} = (L_t + T_t)S_{t-M+1}\). Initial indices are \(S_i = \frac{Y_i}{\text{average}(Y_1, \dots, Y_M)}\).

6.4 · slides 730–732, 740

Stationarity

A series is strictly stationary if the joint distribution of \((Y_{t_1}, \dots, Y_{t_k})\) equals that of \((Y_{t_1 + h}, \dots, Y_{t_k + h})\) for every shift \(h\); it is weakly (covariance) stationary if its mean and variance are constant and \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t+h})\) depends only on the lag \(h\).

6.5 · slides 649–651

Autocovariance and autocorrelation functions

For a stationary series, the autocovariance at lag \(h\) is \(\gamma(h) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t+h})\) and the autocorrelation (ACF) is \(\rho(h) = \frac{\gamma(h)}{\gamma(0)}\), estimated by \(r_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(Y_t - \bar{Y})(Y_{t-k} - \bar{Y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(Y_t - \bar{Y})^2}\).

6.5 · slides 651–656

White noise

A sequence of uncorrelated random variables \(\varepsilon_t\) with mean 0 and constant variance \(\sigma^2\), so \(\rho(h) = 0\) for all \(h \ne 0\); Gaussian white noise is i.i.d. \(N(0, \sigma^2)\).

6.5 · slides 662–665

Partial autocorrelation function (PACF)

The correlation \(\phi_{kk}\) between \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-k}\) after removing the linear effect of the intermediate lags \(Y_{t-1}, \dots, Y_{t-k+1}\); at lag 2, \(\phi_{22} = \frac{\rho_2 - \rho_1^2}{1 - \rho_1^2}\).

6.5 · slides 668–669

Unit root and random walk

A series \(Y_t = \phi Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) has a unit root when \(\phi = 1\); the case \(Y_t = Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) is a random walk, which is non-stationary because its variance grows with \(t\).

6.5 · slides 677–678

Dickey–Fuller test

A unit-root test based on \(\Delta Y_t = \delta Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) (optionally with a constant), \(\delta = \phi - 1\), testing \(H_0: \delta = 0\) (unit root, non-stationary) against \(H_1: \delta < 0\) (stationary) with \(\tau = \frac{\hat{\delta}}{SE(\hat{\delta})}\) compared to Dickey–Fuller critical values.

6.5 · slides 677–680

Differencing

Replacing a series by its changes, \(\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}\) (first difference), \(\Delta^2 Y_t = \Delta Y_t - \Delta Y_{t-1}\), or \(Y_t - Y_{t-m}\) (seasonal difference), to remove trend or seasonality and induce stationarity.

6.5 · slides 688, 765

Autoregressive model AR(p)

A model expressing a stationary series as a linear combination of its own \(p\) previous values plus white noise, \(y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t\); its PACF cuts off after lag \(p\), and for AR(1) the long-run mean is \(\frac{c}{1 - \phi_1}\).

6.6 · slides 670–671, 761

Moving-average model MA(q)

A model expressing a series as a mean plus the current and \(q\) past white-noise shocks, \(y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}\); its ACF cuts off after lag \(q\).

6.6 · slides 683–684, 763

ARMA(p, q) model

A model for a stationary series combining AR and MA parts, \(y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\).

6.6 · slides 684–687, 763

ARIMA(p, d, q) model

An ARMA(p, q) model fitted to a series differenced \(d\) times, \(\Delta^d y_t = c + \sum \phi_i \Delta^d y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum \theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\); forecasts of the differences are integrated (cumulatively summed) back to the original level.

6.6 · slides 688–691, 765

SARIMA and SARIMAX models

SARIMA\((p, d, q)(P, D, Q)_m\) adds seasonal AR, differencing and MA terms at lags that are multiples of the season length \(m\) (12 for monthly data) to an ARIMA model. SARIMAX also includes exogenous regressors \(X_t\) with coefficients \(\beta\), as in \(y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \beta X_t + \varepsilon_t\).

6.6 · slides 767–770

VAR and VARMAX models

VAR(p) models a vector of several series, each regressed on \(p\) lags of itself and of all the others, \(\mathbf{Y}_t = \mathbf{c} + A_1\mathbf{Y}_{t-1} + \dots + A_p\mathbf{Y}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\). VARMAX adds vector moving-average terms and exogenous inputs, \(\dots + \sum_j M_j\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-j} + B\mathbf{X}_t\).

6.6 · slides 771–775

Gaussian mixture model (GMM)

A probabilistic model whose density is a weighted sum of \(K\) Gaussians, \(p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)\), each with mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\), covariance \(\Sigma_k\) and mixing coefficient \(\pi_k\). It is used for soft clustering, density estimation and anomaly detection, where points with very low \(p(\mathbf{x})\) are flagged.

6.7 · slides 780, 784–785, 791

Mixing coefficients and latent variable

The latent variable \(\mathbf{z}\) is an unobserved 1-of-\(K\) indicator of the component that generated a point, with \(z_k \in \{0, 1\}\) and \(\sum_k z_k = 1\). The mixing coefficients are its prior probabilities, \(\pi_k = p(z_k = 1)\), with \(0 \le \pi_k \le 1\) and \(\sum_k \pi_k = 1\).

6.7 · slides 785, 796–799

Responsibility

The posterior probability that component \(k\) generated point \(\mathbf{x}_n\), obtained by Bayes' theorem: \(\gamma(z_{nk}) = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)}\).

6.7 · slides 800, 811

Soft and hard clustering

In soft clustering each point belongs to every cluster with a probability (its responsibilities), so clusters may overlap, as in GMM; in hard clustering each point belongs to exactly one cluster, as in k-means.

6.7 · slides 780, 791

GMM log-likelihood

For data \(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N\), \(\ln p(X \mid \boldsymbol{\pi}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)\); the sum inside the logarithm prevents a closed-form maximum.

6.7 · slides 795, 801

Expectation–maximisation (EM) algorithm

An iterative maximum-likelihood method for latent-variable models: the E-step computes responsibilities from current parameters; the M-step updates \(N_k = \sum_n \gamma(z_{nk})\), \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n \gamma(z_{nk})\mathbf{x}_n\), \(\Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n \gamma(z_{nk})(\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^\top\), \(\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{N}\). It stops when the change in log-likelihood (or parameters) falls below a threshold.

6.7 · slides 787–788, 804–809

Singularity in a GMM

The degenerate maximum-likelihood solution in which a component collapses onto a single data point, its variance tending to 0 and the likelihood to infinity; it is avoided by bounding the variances or by a Bayesian treatment.

6.7 · slide 802

Bayesian information criterion (BIC)

A model-selection score \(BIC = -2\ln \hat{L} + p\ln n\), where \(\hat{L}\) is the maximised likelihood, \(p\) the number of free parameters and \(n\) the sample size; the number of components \(K\) with the smallest BIC is chosen.
