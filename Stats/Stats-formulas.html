M1 · Basic Probability & Statistics · 15 items

Arithmetic mean (ungrouped and grouped)

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}, \qquad \mu = \frac{\sum x_i}{N}, \qquad \bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i} \]

where: \(x_i\) are the observations, \(n\) the sample size, \(N\) the population size, \(f_i\) the frequency of value (or class mid-point) \(x_i\).

use: the balance point of the data. It uses every value, so one outlier can drag it; for grouped classes use the class mid-points as \(x_i\).

1.1.2 · slides 14–17

Sum of squares and the zero-sum of deviations

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0 \]

\[ SS = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum x_i^2 - \frac{\left(\sum x_i\right)^2}{n} \]

where: \(x_i - \bar{x}\) is the deviation of each score from the mean. \(SS\) is the sum of squared deviations from the mean; \(n\) the number of values.

use: explains why the "average deviation" is useless and deviations must be squared (giving SS). Also a quick check on any hand-computed deviation column. The building block of variance, \(\chi^2\), ANOVA and regression. The computational form avoids rounding errors when \(\bar{x}\) is not a whole number.

1.1.2, 1.1.4 · slides 17, 31–37

Median position

\[ \text{Median} = x_{\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)} \ (n \text{ odd}), \qquad \text{Median} = \frac{x_{(n/2)} + x_{(n/2 + 1)}}{2} \ (n \text{ even}) \]

where: \(x_{(k)}\) is the \(k\)-th value after sorting in ascending order.

use: the 50th percentile; robust to outliers. Always sort first. Preferred for skewed or ordinal data.

1.1.2 · slides 20–22

Empirical mode–median–mean relation and skew ordering

\[ \text{Mode} \approx 3\,\text{Median} - 2\,\text{Mean} \]

where: valid for moderately skewed, unimodal data. Right skew: Mean > Median > Mode; left skew: Mean < Median < Mode; symmetric: all three equal.

use: estimate a missing mode or mean, and read the direction of skew from the order of the three measures.

1.1.3 · slide 23

Range

\[ \text{Range} = x_{\max} - x_{\min}, \qquad \text{Range}_{\text{continuous}} = \text{URL}(x_{\max}) - \text{LRL}(x_{\min}) \]

where: URL and LRL are the upper and lower real limits (for example 7 measured to the nearest unit has real limits 6.5 and 7.5).

use: a crude spread measure that depends on only two points, so it is unstable and outlier-sensitive.

1.1.4 · slide 29

Population and sample variance and standard deviation

\[ \sigma^2 = \frac{SS}{N} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \mu)^2}{N}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} \]

\[ s^2 = \frac{SS}{n-1} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}, \qquad s = \sqrt{s^2} \]

where: \(\mu\) is the population mean and \(N\) the population size. \(n - 1\) is the degrees of freedom (one is used up by estimating \(\mu\) with \(\bar{x}\)).

use: only when the data are the whole population. \(\sigma\) is in the data's units; \(\sigma^2\) is in squared units. Use \(n-1\) for a sample: dividing by \(n\) underestimates \(\sigma^2\) because deviations are measured from \(\bar{x}\), which sits closer to the data than \(\mu\) does. Example: \(SS = 24\), \(n = 4\) gives \(s^2 = 8\).

1.1.4 · slides 34–41

Quartile positions, IQR and quartile deviation

\[ \text{Position of } Q_k = \frac{k(n+1)}{4}, \qquad \text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1, \qquad \text{QD} = \frac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2} \]

where: \(k = 1, 2, 3\); a fractional position is interpolated between neighbouring sorted values. QD is the semi-interquartile range.

use: spread of the middle 50%, unaffected by extremes. The webinar instead takes \(Q_1, Q_3\) as medians of the lower and upper halves; both are legitimate, so state your method.

1.1.4, 1.1.5 · slides 43–44

Five-number summary and outlier fences

\[ \text{Inner fences: } Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}, \ \ Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}; \qquad \text{Outer fences: } Q_1 - 3\,\text{IQR}, \ \ Q_3 + 3\,\text{IQR} \]

where: the five-number summary is (Min, \(Q_1\), Median, \(Q_3\), Max).

use: a value beyond an inner fence is a potential (mild) outlier; beyond an outer fence it is an extreme (major) outlier. Box-plot whiskers stop at the most extreme data points inside the inner fences.

1.1.5 · slides 43–48

Classical and empirical probability

\[ P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)}, \qquad P(A) \approx \frac{\text{number of times } A \text{ occurred}}{\text{number of trials}} \]

where: \(n(A)\) is the number of outcomes favourable to \(A\) and \(n(S)\) the number of outcomes in the sample space.

use: classical only when all outcomes are equally likely (fair dice, cards, random selection). Empirical (relative frequency) when you have data instead of symmetry.

1.2.1, 1.2.3 · slides 61–67

Kolmogorov axioms

\[ P(A) \ge 0, \qquad P(S) = 1, \qquad P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i P(A_i) \ \text{ for mutually exclusive } A_i \]

where: \(S\) is the sample space; \(A_i\) are pairwise disjoint events.

use: every probability rule follows from these three. A proposed assignment is invalid if any probability is negative or the total exceeds 1.

1.2.3 · slides 68–69

Complement rule and De Morgan's laws

\[ P(A^c) = 1 - P(A), \qquad (A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c, \qquad (A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c \]

where: \(A^c\) is "not \(A\)".

use: "at least one" problems: \(P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})\). "Neither \(A\) nor \(B\)" is \(P(A^c \cap B^c) = 1 - P(A \cup B)\).

1.2.2, 1.2.4 · slides 63–64

Addition rule and "exactly one"

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

\[ P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \]

\[ P(\text{exactly one}) = P(A) + P(B) - 2P(A \cap B) = P(A \cup B) - P(A \cap B) \]

where: \(P(A \cap B)\) is the probability both occur. "exactly one" is \((A \cap B^c) \cup (A^c \cap B)\).

use: subtract the overlap so it is not counted twice. For mutually exclusive events the intersection terms are 0 and \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\). For independent events this becomes \(P(A)(1 - P(B)) + (1 - P(A))P(B)\), as in "A speaks the truth, B lies" problems.

1.2.4 · slides 70–71, 76–80

Counting with combinations

\[ \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}, \qquad P(\text{exactly } k \text{ of type } A) = \frac{\binom{a}{k}\binom{N-a}{r-k}}{\binom{N}{r}} \]

where: \(n\) objects, choose \(r\) without regard to order; \(N\) items of which \(a\) are of type \(A\), \(r\) drawn without replacement.

use: committees and "defective items in a sample" problems (the hypergeometric count). For "at least one defective", use \(1 - \binom{N-a}{r}/\binom{N}{r}\).

1.2.5 · slides 86–89

Mutually exclusive versus independent

\[ \text{Mutually exclusive: } P(A \cap B) = 0, \qquad \text{Independent: } P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) \]

where: \(A, B\) are events with \(P(A), P(B) > 0\).

use: never confuse the two. If \(P(A), P(B) > 0\), mutually exclusive events are always dependent, because \(0 \ne P(A)P(B)\).

1.2.6 · slides 65, 72–73

Marginals from a joint probability table

\[ P(A_i) = \sum_j P(A_i \cap B_j), \qquad P(B_j) = \sum_i P(A_i \cap B_j), \qquad \sum_i \sum_j P(A_i \cap B_j) = 1 \]

where: \(A_i\) are the row categories and \(B_j\) the column categories of a contingency table; divide counts by the grand total to get probabilities.

use: row and column totals are the marginal probabilities; cell entries are joint probabilities. Independence requires \(P(A_i \cap B_j) = P(A_i)P(B_j)\) in every cell.

M2 · Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem · 13 items

Conditional probability

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}, \qquad P(A) > 0 \]

where: \(P(B \mid A)\) is the probability of \(B\) once \(A\) is known to have occurred; \(A\) becomes the reduced sample space.

use: from a two-way table, divide the cell by its row (or column) total, not by the grand total. \(P(B \mid A) \ne P(A \mid B)\) in general.

2.1.1, 2.1.3 · slides 99–100

Multiplication rule and chain rule

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A), \qquad P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\,P(C \mid A \cap B) \]

where: each factor conditions on everything that came before.

use: sequential draws and tree branches. Without replacement, for example, \(P(\text{two red}) = \frac{r}{N} \cdot \frac{r-1}{N-1}\); with replacement the second factor stays \(\frac{r}{N}\).

2.1.2 · slides 101–108

Independence via conditioning

\[ A, B \text{ independent} \iff P(B \mid A) = P(B) \iff P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) \]

where: the conditions are equivalent when \(P(A) > 0\). If \(A, B\) are independent, so are \(A^c, B\), \(A, B^c\) and \(A^c, B^c\).

use: "at least one of \(n\) independent components works" \(= 1 - \prod_i (1 - p_i)\); a series system works with probability \(\prod_i p_i\).

2.1.4 · slides 109–115

Law of total probability

\[ P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A_i)\,P(B \mid A_i) \]

where: \(A_1, \ldots, A_k\) partition \(S\) (mutually exclusive, exhaustive); \(P(B \mid A_i)\) is the rate of \(B\) within group \(A_i\).

use: overall defect rate across machines, overall positive-test rate, and so on. It is the denominator (evidence) of Bayes' theorem; on a tree, sum the products along every branch ending in \(B\).

2.1.5 · slides 118–121

Bayes' theorem

\[ P(A_i \mid B) = \frac{P(A_i)\,P(B \mid A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} P(A_j)\,P(B \mid A_j)} \]

where: \(P(A_i)\) is the prior, \(P(B \mid A_i)\) the likelihood, the denominator the evidence \(P(B)\), and \(P(A_i \mid B)\) the posterior.

use: reverses a conditional: "which machine produced the defective?", "disease given positive test". The posteriors over the partition always sum to 1.

2.2.1, 2.2.2 · slides 136–137, 148–149, 865–866

Bayes for hypotheses and the posterior odds form

\[ P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)} \propto P(E \mid H)\,P(H) \]

\[ \frac{P(H_1 \mid E)}{P(H_2 \mid E)} = \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)} \times \frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)} \]

where: \(H\) is a hypothesis or class, \(E\) the observed evidence or data. The second factor is the likelihood ratio (Bayes factor).

use: compute the unnormalised score \(P(E \mid H)P(H)\) for each hypothesis, then divide each by their sum to get posteriors. A fast check for two hypotheses that avoids computing \(P(E)\); repeated independent evidence multiplies the likelihood ratios.

2.2.2, 2.2.3 · slides 158–161

Diagnostic test (positive predictive value)

\[ P(D \mid +) = \frac{\text{Se}\cdot\pi}{\text{Se}\cdot\pi + (1 - \text{Sp})(1 - \pi)} \]

where: \(\pi = P(D)\) is the prevalence (base rate), \(\text{Se} = P(+ \mid D)\) the sensitivity, \(\text{Sp} = P(- \mid D^c)\) the specificity, so \(1 - \text{Sp}\) is the false-positive rate.

use: base-rate reasoning. With a rare condition even an accurate test gives a modest \(P(D \mid +)\), because false positives from the large healthy group dominate. The same form covers spam filters and binary channels.

2.2.3 · slides 150–160

MAP and ML hypotheses

\[ h_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{h} P(D \mid h)\,P(h), \qquad h_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_{h} P(D \mid h) \]

where: \(D\) is the training data, \(h\) a candidate hypothesis, \(P(h)\) its prior.

use: MAP uses prior knowledge; ML ignores it. They coincide when the prior is uniform. A strong prior can make MAP and ML choose different hypotheses.

2.2.4 · slides 162–165, 200

Naive (class-conditional) independence assumption

\[ P(X_1, \ldots, X_n \mid Y) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid Y) \]

where: \(X_i\) are the features and \(Y\) the class.

use: turns one huge joint table into \(n\) small ones (parameters grow linearly, not exponentially, in \(n\)). It assumes independence within each class, not overall independence.

2.2.5 · slides 170–171, 881–882

Naive Bayes decision rule and log-space scoring

\[ \hat{y} = \arg\max_{y_k} P(Y = y_k) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i = x_i \mid Y = y_k) \]

\[ \log \text{score}(c) = \log P(c) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P(x_i \mid c) \]

where: \(P(Y = y_k)\) is the class prior and \(P(X_i = x_i \mid Y = y_k)\) the per-feature likelihood. \(\log\) is any fixed base; the argmax is unchanged because \(\log\) is increasing.

use: the evidence \(P(X)\) is the same for every class, so it can be dropped for classification; divide each class score by the sum of scores only if you need actual posterior probabilities. Prevents numerical underflow when many small probabilities are multiplied (long documents). A zero likelihood gives \(\log 0 = -\infty\), which is why smoothing is needed.

2.2.6, 2.2.8 · slides 168–172, 185–199, 886–889

Estimating Naive Bayes probabilities by counting

\[ \hat{P}(Y = c) = \frac{N_c}{N}, \qquad \hat{P}(X_i = v \mid Y = c) = \frac{N_{c,v}}{N_c} \]

where: \(N\) training rows, \(N_c\) rows in class \(c\), \(N_{c,v}\) rows in class \(c\) with feature value \(v\).

use: the frequency table becomes the likelihood table. These counting estimates are exactly the MLEs.

2.2.6 · slides 173–184

Laplace (add-one) smoothing

\[ \hat{P}(X_i = v \mid c) = \frac{N_{c,v} + 1}{N_c + V_i}, \qquad \hat{P}(w \mid c) = \frac{\text{count}(w, c) + 1}{N_c + \lvert V \rvert} \]

where: \(V_i\) is the number of possible values of attribute \(i\); for text, \(N_c\) is the total word count in class \(c\) and \(\lvert V \rvert\) the vocabulary size.

use: fixes the zero-frequency problem (one unseen value would zero the whole product). The denominator must grow by the number of values so the smoothed probabilities still sum to 1.

2.2.7 · slides 177–190

Gaussian Naive Bayes likelihood

\[ P(x_i \mid c) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{c,i}^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x_i - \mu_{c,i})^2}{2\sigma_{c,i}^2}\right) \]

where: \(\mu_{c,i}\) and \(\sigma_{c,i}^2\) are the mean and variance of feature \(i\) within class \(c\).

use: continuous features. The value is a density and may exceed 1; that is fine because only class comparisons matter. Multinomial NB uses word counts and Bernoulli NB uses presence or absence, \(P(x_i \mid c) = p_{ic}^{x_i}(1 - p_{ic})^{1 - x_i}\).

M3a · Random Variables & Joint Distributions · 13 items

Valid probability mass function

\[ p(x) \ge 0 \ \text{ for all } x, \qquad \sum_{x} p(x) = 1 \]

where: \(p(x) = P(X = x)\) for a discrete random variable \(X\).

use: "find \(k\)" problems: set the sum equal to 1 and solve, then check that no \(p(x)\) is negative. For infinite supports use the geometric series \(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} r^j = \frac{1}{1-r}\), \(\lvert r \rvert < 1\).

3.1.1, 3.1.2 · slides 220–226

Expectation of a discrete random variable and linearity

\[ E[X] = \sum_{x} x\,p(x), \qquad E[g(X)] = \sum_{x} g(x)\,p(x) \]

\[ E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b, \qquad E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] \]

where: \(g\) is any function of \(X\), for example \(g(x) = x^2\) or a payoff. \(a, b\) are constants; \(X, Y\) any random variables.

use: long-run average value. A game is fair when the expected net gain is 0. Note \(E[g(X)] \ne g(E[X])\) in general. \(E[X + Y]\) needs no independence. Converts units and payoffs without recomputing the table.

3.1.3 · slides 227–232

Variance (shortcut form) and its linear transform

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) \]

where: \(\mu = E[X]\); \(\sigma_X = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}\).

use: compute \(E[X]\) and \(E[X^2]\) from the table, then subtract. Adding a constant \(b\) does not change the spread; \(\operatorname{SD}(aX + b) = \lvert a \rvert \sigma_X\).

3.1.4 · slides 229–232

Discrete uniform distribution

\[ p(x) = \frac{1}{n}, \ x = 1, \ldots, n; \qquad E[X] = \frac{n+1}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{n^2 - 1}{12} \]

where: \(n\) equally likely integer values.

use: a fair die (\(n = 6\)): mean 3.5, variance \(\frac{35}{12}\). Uses \(\sum k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\) and \(\sum k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\).

3.1.2, 3.1.4 · slides 223–226

Cumulative distribution function

\[ F(x) = P(X \le x) = \sum_{t \le x} p(t), \qquad P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a) \]

where: \(F\) is non-decreasing, \(F(-\infty) = 0\), \(F(\infty) = 1\); for a discrete \(X\) it is a step function that jumps by \(p(x)\) at each \(x\).

use: for discrete \(X\), endpoints matter: \(P(X < b) = F(b) - p(b)\). Recover the pmf as \(p(x) = F(x) - F(x^-)\).

3.1.5 · slides 233–239

Valid probability density function and its CDF

\[ f(x) \ge 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1, \qquad P(a \le X \le b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx \]

\[ F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt, \qquad f(x) = F'(x), \qquad F(m) = 0.5 \ \text{(median)} \]

where: \(f\) is the pdf of a continuous \(X\). \(m\) is the median; the \(p\)-th percentile solves \(F(x_p) = p\).

use: probability is area. \(P(X = a) = 0\), so \(<\) and \(\le\) give the same answer. \(f(x)\) itself can exceed 1 (for example \(\text{Uniform}(0, 0.25)\) has \(f = 4\)). For piecewise pdfs, integrate each piece over its own interval. Differentiate a given CDF to get the pdf; solve \(F(x) = p\) for medians and percentiles.

3.1.5, 3.1.6 · slides 240–247

Mean and variance of a continuous random variable

\[ E[X] = \int x f(x)\,dx, \qquad E[X^2] = \int x^2 f(x)\,dx, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 \]

where: integrals run over the support of \(f\). Power rule: \(\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\).

use: same shortcut as the discrete case with sums replaced by integrals. For \(\text{Uniform}(a, b)\): \(f = \frac{1}{b-a}\), \(E[X] = \frac{a+b}{2}\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}\).

3.1.6 · slides 244–249

Joint distribution validity

\[ \sum_{x}\sum_{y} p(x, y) = 1, \qquad \iint f(x, y)\,dx\,dy = 1, \qquad P((X, Y) \in A) = \iint_A f(x, y)\,dx\,dy \]

where: \(p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)\) is the joint pmf; \(f(x, y)\) the joint pdf; both must be non-negative.

use: "find \(k\)" for joint tables or densities. For non-rectangular regions (such as \(0 < y < x < 1\)) set the inner integration limits from the region.

3.1.7 · slides 251–254, 907–909

Marginal distributions

\[ p_X(x) = \sum_{y} p(x, y), \qquad f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y)\,dy \]

where: sum or integrate out the other variable (row and column totals of a joint table).

use: needed before testing independence or computing \(E[X]\), \(E[Y]\) separately.

3.1.7 · slides 255–256

Conditional distribution and conditional expectation

\[ p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p_X(x)}, \qquad f(y \mid x) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_X(x)}, \qquad E[Y \mid X = x] = \sum_y y\,p(y \mid x) \]

where: \(p_X(x) > 0\) (or \(f_X(x) > 0\)); use \(\int y f(y \mid x)\,dy\) in the continuous case.

use: a slice of the joint table renormalised to sum to 1.

3.1.7 · slides 258–262

Independence of random variables

\[ X, Y \text{ independent} \iff p(x, y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y) \ \ (\text{or } f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y)) \ \text{ for all } (x, y) \]

where: the product must hold in every cell; one failing cell proves dependence.

use: a support that is not a rectangle (for example a triangle) already implies dependence.

3.1.7 · slides 257, 911

Covariance from a joint distribution

\[ E[XY] = \sum_x \sum_y xy\,p(x, y), \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X]\,E[Y] \]

where: \(E[X]\) and \(E[Y]\) come from the marginals.

use: independence implies \(\operatorname{Cov} = 0\), but not conversely: \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\), \(Y = X^2\) has \(\operatorname{Cov} = 0\) yet \(Y\) is determined by \(X\).

3.1.7, 5.1 · slides 547, 914–917

Variance of a sum or difference

\[ \operatorname{Var}(aX \pm bY) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X) + b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y) \pm 2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) \]

where: \(a, b\) constants.

use: for independent \(X, Y\) the covariance term vanishes, and variances add even for a difference: \(\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)\). This is why the SE of \(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2\) adds the two variances.

3.1.4, 3.1.7 · slides 229–232, 547
M3b · Standard Distributions, Sampling, CLT & Estimation · 22 items

Bernoulli distribution

\[ P(X = x) = p^x q^{1-x}, \ x \in \{0, 1\}; \qquad E[X] = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = pq \]

where: \(p\) is the probability of success and \(q = 1 - p\).

use: a single yes/no trial. \(pq\) is largest (0.25) at \(p = 0.5\), which is why \(p = 0.5\) is the conservative choice in sample-size formulas.

3.2.1 · slides 278–282

Binomial distribution: pmf, mean and variance

\[ P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x}, \qquad x = 0, 1, \ldots, n \]

\[ E[X] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = npq, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{npq} \]

where: \(n\) fixed trials, \(p\) constant success probability, \(q = 1 - p\), trials independent, each with two outcomes (the four conditions). \(X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)\) is a sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli\((p)\) variables.

use: count of successes in \(n\) trials. Use the complement for "at least": \(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - q^n\). Sampling without replacement from a small population breaks the conditions (hypergeometric instead). Inverse problems: given mean and variance, \(q = \frac{\operatorname{Var}}{\text{mean}}\), then \(p = 1 - q\) and \(n = \frac{\text{mean}}{p}\). Smallest \(n\) for \(P(\text{at least one}) \ge c\): \(n \ge \frac{\ln(1-c)}{\ln q}\), rounded up.

3.2.2 · slides 283–292

Poisson distribution: pmf, rate scaling, mean and variance

\[ P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}, \quad x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots; \qquad \lambda = \text{rate} \times \text{exposure} \]

\[ E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\lambda} \]

where: \(\lambda\) is the expected count in the interval of interest (time, area, length). \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\).

use: counts of rare, independent events at a constant rate. Rescale \(\lambda\) to the interval asked about: 5 defects per 10 sq ft means \(\lambda = 7.5\) for 15 sq ft. Recursion: \(P(x+1) = P(x)\frac{\lambda}{x+1}\). Mean = variance is the Poisson fingerprint; sample variance much larger than the mean signals overdispersion. Independent Poisson counts add: \(\lambda_{\text{total}} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2\).

where: \(n\) large and \(p\) small.

use: the slides' condition is \(n \ge 100\) and \(np \le 10\). Example: 3% defective, \(n = 200\), so \(\lambda = 6\).

Normal probability density

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad -\infty < x < \infty \]

where: \(\mu\) is the mean (= median = mode), \(\sigma\) the standard deviation; \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\).

use: symmetric bell curve, total area 1. Probabilities come from the Z-table after standardising, never from \(f(x)\) directly.

Standardisation (z-score) and its inverse

\[ Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0, 1), \qquad x = \mu + z\sigma \]

where: \(\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z)\); symmetry gives \(\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)\).

use: \(P(a < X < b) = \Phi\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right) - \Phi\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\). The inverse form gives percentiles and cut-offs; two known percentiles give two linear equations for \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).

where: \(X\) normal.

Linear combinations of normals

where: \(X, Y\) independent normals for the second result.

use: totals, differences and unit conversions of normal quantities stay normal; variances add for both sums and differences.

Normal approximation to the binomial with continuity correction

\[ X \approx N(np, \ npq), \qquad P(X \le k) \approx \Phi\left(\frac{k + 0.5 - np}{\sqrt{npq}}\right), \qquad P(X \ge k) \approx 1 - \Phi\left(\frac{k - 0.5 - np}{\sqrt{npq}}\right) \]

Chi-square distribution

\[ \chi^2_k = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2, \qquad E[\chi^2_k] = k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\chi^2_k) = 2k \]

where: \(Z_i\) independent \(N(0, 1)\); \(k\) the degrees of freedom; support \([0, \infty)\), right-skewed.

use: sampling distribution of variances and of the \(\chi^2\) test statistics. For normal samples \(\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\).

3.2.6 · slides 322–323

Student's t distribution

\[ t_k = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{\chi^2_k / k}}, \qquad \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1} \]

where: \(Z\) and \(\chi^2_k\) independent; \(k\) degrees of freedom.

use: replaces \(z\) when \(\sigma\) is unknown and estimated by \(s\) (normal population). Heavier tails than \(N(0, 1)\), so critical values are larger; \(t_k \to N(0, 1)\) as \(k \to \infty\).

3.2.6 · slides 324–326

F distribution

\[ F_{\nu_1, \nu_2} = \frac{\chi^2_{\nu_1}/\nu_1}{\chi^2_{\nu_2}/\nu_2}, \qquad \frac{s_1^2/\sigma_1^2}{s_2^2/\sigma_2^2} \sim F_{n_1 - 1,\, n_2 - 1}, \qquad F_{1-\alpha}(\nu_1, \nu_2) = \frac{1}{F_{\alpha}(\nu_2, \nu_1)} \]

where: \(\nu_1\) numerator and \(\nu_2\) denominator degrees of freedom; independent chi-squares.

use: ratio of variances (F-test, ANOVA). The order of the degrees of freedom matters. Also \(t_k^2 = F_{1, k}\).

3.2.6 · slides 327–328

Sampling design: interval, allocation and error decomposition

\[ k = \frac{N}{n}, \qquad n_h = n \cdot \frac{N_h}{N} \]

\[ \text{Observed value} = \text{Truth} + \text{Bias} + \text{Random error} \]

where: \(k\) is the sampling interval (pick a random start in \(1..k\), then every \(k\)-th unit); \(N_h\) is the size of stratum \(h\) and \(n_h\) its share of the total sample \(n\). Bias is systematic error from the design (non-random selection, measurement); random error is sampling variation.

use: systematic needs an ordered frame without periodicity; stratified suits a heterogeneous population split into homogeneous strata. Round \(n_h\) so the strata still add to \(n\). Larger \(n\) shrinks random error only; bias needs a better design (probability sampling).

3.3.1, 3.3.2, 3.3.3 · slides 349–365

Sampling distribution of the sample mean

\[ E[\bar{x}] = \mu, \qquad SE(\bar{x}) = \sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where: \(\sigma\) is the population standard deviation and \(n\) the sample size.

use: the mean of \(\bar{x}\) never depends on \(n\); only the spread shrinks, like \(1/\sqrt{n}\) (quadrupling \(n\) halves the SE).

3.3.4 · slides 366–375

Finite population correction

\[ \sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}}, \qquad \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{PQ}{n}}\sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}} \]

where: \(N\) is the population size; \(\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}\) is the FPC.

use: apply when sampling without replacement and \(n/N \ge 0.05\). The FPC tends to 1 for large \(N\) and equals 0 when \(n = N\).

3.3.4 · slides 380–382

Central Limit Theorem (z for a sample mean)

\[ \bar{x} \approx N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right), \qquad Z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \]

where: any population with finite variance; \(n\) large (rule of thumb \(n \ge 30\)); exact for any \(n\) when the population is normal.

use: probabilities about averages. A single value far from \(\mu\) may be ordinary while the same value as a mean of \(n\) is unusual, because the SE is \(\sqrt{n}\) times smaller.

3.3.5 · slides 371–379

Sampling distribution of the sample proportion

\[ E[\hat{p}] = P, \qquad SE(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{PQ}{n}}, \qquad Z = \frac{\hat{p} - P}{\sqrt{PQ/n}} \]

where: \(\hat{p} = x/n\), \(P\) the population proportion, \(Q = 1 - P\).

use: normal shape when \(nP > 15\) and \(nQ > 15\) (course rule). Use \(P\) (the hypothesised or known value) in the SE for probability and test questions.

3.3.6 · slides 383–387

Bias, unbiasedness and mean squared error of an estimator

\[ \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E[\hat{\theta}] - \theta, \qquad \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = E[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2 \]

where: \(\hat{\theta}\) is an estimator of the parameter \(\theta\).

use: unbiased means bias 0 (\(\bar{x}\), \(s^2\) with \(n-1\), \(\hat{p}\)); consistent means \(\hat{\theta} \to \theta\) as \(n \to \infty\); the more efficient of two unbiased estimators has the smaller variance. \(E\left[\frac{SS}{n}\right] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\), so dividing by \(n\) is biased.

\[ \bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where: \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the upper \(\alpha/2\) point of \(N(0, 1)\): 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.326 (98%), 2.576 (99%). \(s\) is the sample SD and \(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\) the t critical value with \(n - 1\) df.

use: \(\sigma\) known, or \(n \ge 30\) with \(s\) in place of \(\sigma\) (large-sample interval). "95% confidence" describes the procedure: 95% of such intervals capture the fixed \(\mu\). Small samples from a roughly normal population. Wider than the z interval at the same confidence.

where: \(\hat{p} = x/n\), \(\hat{q} = 1 - \hat{p}\).

use: large samples (\(n\hat{p}\) and \(n\hat{q}\) comfortably large). In an interval use \(\hat{p}\) in the SE, since \(P\) is unknown.

3.3.8 · slides 383–394

Test statistic, p-value and decision rule

\[ \text{Test statistic} = \frac{\text{estimate} - \text{value under } H_0}{\text{standard error under } H_0} \]

\[ p_{\text{right}} = P(Z \ge z_{\text{obs}}), \qquad p_{\text{left}} = P(Z \le z_{\text{obs}}), \qquad p_{\text{two}} = 2P(Z \ge \lvert z_{\text{obs}} \rvert) \]

where: \(H_0\) holds the equality (=, \(\le\) or \(\ge\)); \(H_1\) is the claim to be established (\(\ne\), \(>\) or \(<\)). Probabilities computed assuming \(H_0\) is true (use the t, \(\chi^2\) or F distribution for those tests).

use: every z and t test in this module is this ratio. The direction of \(H_1\) sets the tail: "more than, exceeds" is right-tailed, "less than, at most" is left-tailed, "differs, changed" is two-tailed. Reject \(H_0\) if \(p \le \alpha\), equivalently if the statistic falls in the critical region. The p-value is \(P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)\), not \(P(H_0 \text{ true})\); failing to reject does not prove \(H_0\).

Type I error, Type II error and power

\[ \alpha = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true}), \qquad \beta = P(\text{fail to reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ true}), \qquad \text{Power} = 1 - \beta \]

where: \(m\) independent tests, each at level \(\alpha\); FWER is the chance of at least one false rejection.

5.1 · slides 541–546

Covariance rules and the variance–covariance matrix

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(aX + b, \ cY + d) = ac\,\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y), \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X), \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{Var}(X_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(X_2, X_1) & \operatorname{Var}(X_2) \end{bmatrix} \]

where: \(a, b, c, d\) constants; \(\Sigma\) is symmetric with variances on the diagonal and covariances off it (for \(p\) variables it is \(p \times p\)).

use: shifts do not change covariance; rescaling multiplies it, which is why covariance is unit-dependent. \(\Sigma\) is the input to PCA and to multivariate Gaussians.

5.1 · slides 543–548

Principal components from the covariance matrix

\[ \det(\Sigma - \lambda I) = 0, \qquad (\Sigma - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \text{share of variance of } PC_1 = \frac{\lambda_1}{\sum_j \lambda_j} \]

where: \(\lambda_j\) are the eigenvalues (variances along the principal directions) and \(\mathbf{v}\) the unit eigenvectors. For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, \(\lambda = \frac{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma \pm \sqrt{(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)^2 - 4\det\Sigma}}{2}\).

use: checks: \(\sum \lambda_j = \operatorname{trace}(\Sigma)\) and \(\prod \lambda_j = \det(\Sigma)\). Example: \(\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) has \(\lambda = 6, 1\), so \(PC_1\) explains \(6/7 = 85.7\%\). PCs are uncorrelated because the eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal.

Pearson correlation coefficient and strength bands

\[ r = \frac{s_{xy}}{s_x s_y} = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2 \sum (y - \bar{y})^2}} = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x \sum y}{\sqrt{\left[n\sum x^2 - \left(\sum x\right)^2\right]\left[n\sum y^2 - \left(\sum y\right)^2\right]}} \]

\[ 0 < \lvert r \rvert \le 0.25 \ \text{weak}, \qquad 0.25 < \lvert r \rvert \le 0.75 \ \text{moderate}, \qquad 0.75 < \lvert r \rvert < 1 \ \text{strong}, \qquad \lvert r \rvert = 1 \ \text{perfect} \]

where: \(s_x, s_y\) are the standard deviations (the divisor cancels, so \(n\) or \(n - 1\) gives the same \(r\)). The sign gives the direction (positive or negative correlation).

use: strength and direction of linear association, \(-1 \le r \le 1\), unit-free. \(r = 0\) means no linear relationship (\(y = x^2\) on a symmetric range has \(r = 0\)); correlation is not causation. Verbal interpretation in exam answers; always pair it with a scatter plot reading, because a single outlier or a curved pattern can distort \(r\).

\[ b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y})}{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x \sum y}{n\sum x^2 - \left(\sum x\right)^2}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x} \]

The two regression lines and their properties

\[ y - \bar{y} = b_{yx}(x - \bar{x}), \quad b_{yx} = r\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x, y)}{\sigma_x^2}; \qquad x - \bar{x} = b_{xy}(y - \bar{y}), \quad b_{xy} = r\frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x, y)}{\sigma_y^2} \]

\[ r^2 = b_{yx}\,b_{xy}, \qquad r = \operatorname{sign}(b_{yx})\sqrt{b_{yx}\,b_{xy}}, \qquad \text{both lines pass through } (\bar{x}, \bar{y}) \]

where: Y on X predicts \(y\) from \(x\); X on Y predicts \(x\) from \(y\). \(b_{yx}\), \(b_{xy}\) and \(r\) always share the same sign.

use: use Y on X to estimate \(y\) and X on Y to estimate \(x\); they are different lines unless \(r = \pm 1\). where: SSR is the explained (regression) variation, SSE the residual variation; in simple regression \(r^2\) equals the square of Pearson's \(r\) and \(\text{SSR} = b\,S_{xy}\).

Simple and weighted moving averages

\[ \text{MA}_t(k) = \frac{Y_t + Y_{t-1} + \cdots + Y_{t-k+1}}{k}, \qquad F_{t+1} = \text{MA}_t(k) \]

\[ F_{t+1} = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} w_j Y_{t-j}, \qquad \sum_j w_j = 1 \]

where: \(k\) is the window length; as a forecast, the average of the last \(k\) values predicts the next period. As a trend, an odd \(k\) is placed at the middle of its window. \(w_0\) is the weight on the most recent value (usually the largest).

use: smooths out irregular (and, with \(k = M\), seasonal) variation. Larger \(k\) is smoother but lags a trend more: on a linear trend with slope \(b\) the trailing forecast lags by \(\frac{b(k+1)}{2}\). Reacts faster than a simple MA. Example: weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 on 141, 132, 120 give 134.1.

Forecast error and accuracy measures

\[ e_t = Y_t - F_t, \quad \text{MSE} = \frac{\sum e_t^2}{n}, \quad \text{MAD} = \frac{\sum \lvert e_t \rvert}{n}, \quad \text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n}\sum \left\lvert \frac{e_t}{Y_t} \right\rvert, \quad \text{LAD} = \max_t \lvert e_t \rvert \]

Simple exponential smoothing and its weights

\[ F_{t+1} = \alpha Y_t + (1 - \alpha)F_t = F_t + \alpha e_t, \qquad F_2 = Y_1 \]

\[ F_{t+1} = \sum_{j=0}^{t-2} \alpha(1 - \alpha)^j Y_{t-j} + (1 - \alpha)^{t-1}F_2 \]

where: \(0 \le \alpha \le 1\) is the smoothing constant; the course initialises \(F_2 = Y_1\). The weights \(\alpha, \alpha(1-\alpha), \alpha(1-\alpha)^2, \ldots\) decay geometrically and, with the initial weight, sum to 1.

use: level-only series (no trend, no seasonality). \(\alpha \to 1\) gives the naive forecast \(F_{t+1} = Y_t\); \(\alpha \to 0\) freezes the forecast. Choose \(\alpha\) by out-of-sample MSE or MAD. On a trending series SES lags, which motivates Holt. Explains the name "exponential". The average age of the data used is \(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\) periods, so small \(\alpha\) means a long memory.

Autocovariance, autocorrelation and weak stationarity

\[ \gamma(h) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t+h}) = E[(Y_t - \mu)(Y_{t+h} - \mu)], \qquad \rho(h) = \frac{\gamma(h)}{\gamma(0)} \]

where: \(h\) is the lag, \(\gamma(0) = \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)\). Weakly stationary means \(E[Y_t] = \mu\) constant, \(E[Y_t^2] < \infty\), and \(\gamma\) depends only on \(h\), not on \(t\).

use: stationarity is required before fitting AR, MA or ARMA models. A trend (changing mean) or growing variance breaks it.

where: \(T\) is the series length, \(k\) the lag, \(\bar{Y}\) the overall mean.

Dickey–Fuller unit-root test

\[ Y_t = \phi Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \ \Rightarrow \ \Delta Y_t = \delta Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \quad \delta = \phi - 1; \qquad \tau = \frac{\hat{\delta}}{\text{SE}(\hat{\delta})} \]

Differencing and ARIMA(p, d, q)

\[ BY_t = Y_{t-1}, \qquad \nabla Y_t = (1 - B)Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}, \qquad \nabla^2 Y_t = Y_t - 2Y_{t-1} + Y_{t-2}, \qquad \nabla_m Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-m} \]

AR(p) model and AR(1) properties

\[ Y_t = c + \phi_1 Y_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_p Y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t; \qquad \text{AR(1): } \mu = \frac{c}{1 - \phi}, \quad \gamma(0) = \frac{\sigma^2}{1 - \phi^2}, \quad \rho(h) = \phi^h \]

where: \(\phi_i\) are the AR coefficients, \(\varepsilon_t\) white noise with variance \(\sigma^2\); AR(1) is stationary iff \(\lvert \phi \rvert < 1\).

use: identify by a PACF cut-off at lag \(p\) and a geometrically decaying ACF. Example: \(c = 5\), \(\phi = 0.6\), \(\sigma^2 = 9\) gives \(\mu = 12.5\), variance 14.06. Coefficients are estimated by least squares or MLE on lagged values.

MA(q) model and MA(1) autocorrelation

\[ Y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}; \qquad \text{MA(1): } \rho_1 = \frac{\theta}{1 + \theta^2}, \quad \rho_h = 0 \ (h \ge 2) \]

where: \(\theta_j\) are the MA coefficients on past shocks.

use: identify by an ACF cut-off after lag \(q\). Every finite MA is stationary; for an MA(1), \(\lvert \rho_1 \rvert \le 0.5\), and \(\theta\) and \(1/\theta\) give the same \(\rho_1\) (report the invertible one, \(\lvert \theta \rvert < 1\)).

ARMA(p, q) and the recursive forecasting rule

\[ Y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i Y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}; \qquad \hat{Y}_{t+h} \text{: replace future } Y \text{ by forecasts and future } \varepsilon \text{ by } 0 \]

where: past residuals \(\varepsilon_t\) (from the fit) are kept; \(E[\varepsilon_{t+h}] = 0\) for \(h \ge 1\).

use: example: \(Y_t = 3 + 0.5Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t + 0.4\varepsilon_{t-1}\), \(Y_{100} = 10\), \(\varepsilon_{100} = 2\) gives \(\hat{Y}_{101} = 8.8\), then \(\hat{Y}_{102} = 3 + 0.5(8.8) = 7.4\). Long-run forecasts approach the mean \(\frac{c}{1 - \sum \phi_i}\).

SARIMA, SARIMAX and VAR

\[ \Phi_P(B^m)\,\phi_p(B)\,\nabla^d\nabla_m^D\,Y_t = \Theta_Q(B^m)\,\theta_q(B)\,\varepsilon_t \ \ (+\,\beta X_t \text{ for SARIMAX}); \qquad \mathbf{Y}_t = \mathbf{c} + A_1\mathbf{Y}_{t-1} + \cdots + A_p\mathbf{Y}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t \]

where: \((p, d, q)\) non-seasonal and \((P, D, Q)_m\) seasonal orders with period \(m\); \(X_t\) exogenous regressors with effect \(\beta\); in VAR(\(p\)), \(\mathbf{Y}_t\) is a vector and the matrices \(A_i\) hold own- and cross-variable lag effects.

use: SARIMA for repeating seasonal patterns, SARIMAX when known drivers (price, promotion) matter, VAR (or VARMAX with exogenous inputs) for several series that feed back on each other. Forecast with the same recursive rule.

Gaussian densities (univariate and multivariate)

\[ \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \qquad \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\lvert \Sigma \rvert^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right) \]

where: \(d\) is the dimension, \(\Sigma\) the covariance matrix, \(\lvert \Sigma \rvert\) its determinant.

use: the component density of a GMM. When components share \(\sigma\), the constant cancels in responsibilities and only the exponents matter.

Gaussian mixture density and latent variable

\[ p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad 0 \le \pi_k \le 1, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k = 1, \qquad p(z_k = 1) = \pi_k \]

where: \(\pi_k\) are the mixing coefficients (priors), \(\mathbf{z}\) a 1-of-\(K\) latent indicator with \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid z_k = 1) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)\), so \(p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}} p(\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})\).

use: soft clustering and density estimation. Example: \(\pi = (0.7, 0.3)\), component densities 0.06476 and 0.08066 at \(x = 13\) give \(p(13) = 0.06953\).

Responsibilities (E-step, Bayes' theorem)

\[ \gamma(z_{nk}) = p(z_k = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_n) = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)} \]

where: \(\pi_k\) is the prior, \(\mathcal{N}(\cdot)\) the likelihood and \(\gamma(z_{nk})\) the posterior probability that component \(k\) generated point \(n\); \(\sum_k \gamma(z_{nk}) = 1\).

use: the E-step. Example: \(\pi = (0.6, 0.4)\), \(\mu = (90, 100)\), \(\sigma = 5\), \(x = 95\) is equidistant, so \(\gamma_1 = 0.6\). A hard assignment (argmax) is a separate, optional step.

GMM log-likelihood

\[ \ln p(X \mid \boldsymbol{\pi}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)\right\} \]

where: \(N\) data points, \(K\) components.

use: the sum sits inside the log, so setting derivatives to zero gives coupled equations with no closed form, hence EM. Track it each iteration to monitor convergence; it can blow up to \(+\infty\) if a component collapses onto one point (singularity).

M-step updates

\[ N_k = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma(z_{nk}), \quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n}\gamma(z_{nk})\,\mathbf{x}_n, \quad \Sigma_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n}\gamma(z_{nk})(\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\text{new}})(\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\text{new}})^\top, \quad \pi_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{N_k}{N} \]

where: \(N_k\) is the effective number of points in component \(k\); in 1-D, \(\sigma_k^2 = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n \gamma(z_{nk})(x_n - \mu_k)^2\).

use: responsibility-weighted versions of the ordinary MLEs. Erratum (CS-15 Problem 2): \(\mu_1 = \frac{0.83(92) + 0.60(95) + 0.31(98)}{1.74} \approx 94.1\) and \(\pi_1 = \frac{0.83 + 0.60 + 0.31}{3} = 0.58\) (the slide omits the division by \(N = 3\)).

EM loop and convergence

\[ \ell^{(t+1)} \ge \ell^{(t)}, \qquad \text{stop when } \left\lvert \ell^{(t+1)} - \ell^{(t)} \right\rvert < \epsilon \ \text{ (or parameters stop changing)} \]

where: \(\ell^{(t)}\) is the log-likelihood after iteration \(t\); \(\epsilon\) a small tolerance.

use: initialise \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k, \pi_k\) (often from k-means), then alternate E-step and M-step. Each cycle never decreases \(\ell\), but EM can stop at a local optimum, so use several restarts and a variance floor against singularities.

Choosing K with BIC and AIC

\[ \text{BIC} = -2\ell + p\ln n, \qquad \text{AIC} = -2\ell + 2p, \qquad p_{\text{1-D GMM}} = 3K - 1 \]

where: \(\ell\) is the maximised log-likelihood, \(n\) the number of points, \(p\) the number of free parameters (\(K\) means, \(K\) variances, \(K - 1\) free weights in 1-D).

use: fit several \(K\) and choose the smallest criterion. BIC's penalty (\(\ln n\) per parameter) exceeds AIC's (2 per parameter) once \(n > 7\), so BIC favours fewer components.
