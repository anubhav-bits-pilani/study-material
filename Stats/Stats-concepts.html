M1 · Basic Probability & Statistics · 21 items

Types of data

Data are either categorical (qualitative: eye colour, party) or numerical (quantitative). Numerical data are discrete when counted (number of children, defects per hour) and continuous when measured (weight, voltage). The type decides which summaries, plots and tests are legitimate.

1.1.1 · slides 9, 11–12

Levels of measurement

Nominal < ordinal < interval < ratio: each level adds a property (order, meaningful differences, a true zero). Only a ratio scale has a true zero, so "twice as heavy" makes sense for kilograms but "twice as hot" does not for °C or °F, which are interval scales.

1.1.1 · slides 10–12

The mean as a balance point

The mean \(\bar{x} = \sum x / n\) (or \(\sum fx / \sum f\) for grouped data) uses every value, which makes it stable across samples but sensitive to outliers. Deviations from it always sum to zero, \(\sum (x - \bar{x}) = 0\), which is why it is called the balance point and why "mean deviation" is useless as a spread measure.

1.1.2 · slides 14–17, 31–32

Median and mode

The median is the middle value (odd \(n\)) or the average of the two middle values (even \(n\)); for 29, 31, 35, 39, 39, 40, 43, 44, 44, 52 it is \((39 + 40)/2 = 39.5\). It is the 50th percentile and ignores outliers. The mode is the most frequent value, the only centre valid for nominal data; a set can be unimodal, bimodal, multimodal or have no mode.

1.1.2 · slides 18–22, 822–824

Choosing a measure of centre

Use the mean for interval/ratio data without extreme values, the median for ordinal data or heavily skewed data, and the mode for nominal data. Reporting income or house prices by the mean is the classic mistake, because a few large values drag it up.

1.1.2, 1.1.3 · slides 17, 19, 21, 23

Shape: symmetry and skewness

In a symmetric distribution mean = median = mode. A long right tail (positive skew) pulls the mean above the median (mean > median > mode) and a long left tail gives mean < median < mode, so comparing mean and median is a quick skewness check. For moderate skew the empirical relation \(\text{Mode} \approx 3\,\text{Median} - 2\,\text{Mean}\) holds.

1.1.3 · slide 23

Why spread matters; the range

The deck's two groups both have mean, median and mode 5, yet one contains a 15, so a centre alone cannot say how typical it is. The simplest spread measure, range = largest − smallest (real limits for continuous data), uses only two points, so one outlier can change it completely.

1.1.4 · slides 24–29

Sum of squares, variance and standard deviation

Raw deviations cancel, so square them: \(SS = \sum (x - \bar{x})^2\). Variance is the average squared deviation and the SD its square root, back in the data's units; the deck's group 1 has \(s = 2.345\) and group 2 (with the 15) \(s = 4.093\). Together \(\bar{x}\) and \(s\) summarise a distribution in two numbers, the basis of the \(z\)-score.

1.1.4 · slides 30–37, 42

Why divide by \(n - 1\)

Sample deviations are measured from \(\bar{x}\), which sits closer to the data than \(\mu\) does, so \(SS\) is too small and \(SS/n\) underestimates \(\sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n - 1\) (the degrees of freedom) makes \(s^2\) unbiased: a sample of four scores with \(SS = 24\) has \(s^2 = 24/3 = 8\), not 6.

1.1.4 · slides 38–41

IQR and quartile deviation

\(\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1\) is the spread of the middle 50% and is not affected by extreme values; \(QD = \text{IQR}/2\). For 11, 12, 13, 16, 16, 17, 17, 18, 21: \(Q_1 = 12.5\), \(Q_3 = 17.5\), IQR = 5, QD = 2.5.

1.1.4 · slide 44

Five-number summary and quartile methods

Min, \(Q_1\), median, \(Q_3\), max. The CS-1 slides find \(Q_k\) at position \(k(n+1)/4\); the webinar takes the median of each half. The two methods can give slightly different quartiles, so state the method in an exam.

1.1.5 · slides 43–44, 829, 834–835

Fences, outliers and the box plot

Values below \(Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}\) or above \(Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}\) are potential outliers (beyond \(\pm 3\,\text{IQR}\), extreme); for 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 18, 30 the upper fence is 25.5, so 30 is an outlier. The box plot draws \(Q_1\), median and \(Q_3\) as a box, with whiskers to the most extreme values inside the fences; an off-centre median or one long whisker shows skew, and side-by-side boxes compare groups.

1.1.5 · slides 45–48, 831–832, 835

Reading a describe() table

A summary table (count, mean, std, min, quartiles, max) is a data-quality check: a min of 0 study hours, 12 hours of screen time or 35% attendance should be verified as possible entry errors before modelling. Compare mean with median and std with the range to spot skew and outliers.

1.1.5 · slides 836–839

Random experiment, sample space and events

A random experiment is any action whose outcome is not known in advance. The sample space \(S\) is the set of all outcomes (a die gives \(S = \{1, \dots, 6\}\)), and an event is any subset of \(S\), including \(\varnothing\) and \(S\) itself. An event "occurs" when the outcome lies in it.

1.2.1 · slides 59–62

Event algebra and De Morgan

Complement \(A^c\) (not \(A\)), union \(A \cup B\) (either or both) and intersection \(A \cap B\) (both) are read off a Venn diagram. De Morgan's laws, \((A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c\) and \((A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c\), turn "neither" into "not either".

1.2.2 · slides 63–65, 71

Three definitions of probability

Classical: \(P(A) = n(A)/n(S)\) for equally likely outcomes. Empirical: the long-run relative frequency of \(A\). Axiomatic (Kolmogorov): any assignment with \(P(A) \ge 0\), \(P(S) = 1\) and additivity over disjoint events, so values over exhaustive outcomes must be non-negative and sum to exactly 1 (0.38, 0.16, 0.11, 0.35 is legal; a set with −0.06 is not).

1.2.3 · slides 66–69, 74–75

Addition and complement rules

\(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\): the overlap is counted twice and must be removed once. The rule also runs backwards: 75% in annuities, 45% in stocks and 85% in at least one give 35% in both. The complement rule \(P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)\) handles "not" questions.

1.2.4 · slides 70, 80–81

"At least one", "exactly one" and "neither"

"At least one" is \(1 - P(\text{none})\), usually far easier than adding cases. "Exactly one" is \(P(A) + P(B) - 2P(A \cap B)\), and "neither" is \(1 - P(A \cup B)\) by De Morgan. Two witnesses who tell the truth 80% and 60% of the time contradict each other with probability \(0.32 + 0.12 = 0.44\).

1.2.4 · slides 78–79, 88, 841–843

Counting with combinations

When a group is drawn without regard to order, count with \(\binom{n}{r}\) and divide favourable selections by total selections. A committee of 5 from 8 men and 4 women has a female majority with probability \(\frac{\binom{8}{1}\binom{4}{4} + \binom{8}{2}\binom{4}{3}}{\binom{12}{5}} = \frac{120}{792} = \frac{5}{33}\).

1.2.5 · slides 86, 89

Mutually exclusive vs independent

Mutually exclusive means \(P(A \cap B) = 0\) (cannot occur together); independent means \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\) (no information about each other), checked numerically. If \(P(A), P(B) > 0\), disjoint events are necessarily dependent, since learning \(A\) happened tells you \(B\) did not; so "mutually exclusive implies independent" is false.

1.2.6 · slides 65, 72–73, 84–85, 844–845

Joint probability (contingency) tables

A two-way table of counts or proportions gives joint probabilities in its cells and marginal probabilities in its row and column totals. Dividing a cell by the grand total gives \(P(A \cap B)\); dividing by a row or column total gives a conditional probability (M2).

1.2.7 · slides 91, 102–103
M2 · Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem · 18 items

Conditional probability as a reduced sample space

\(P(B \mid A) = P(A \cap B)/P(A)\): once \(A\) is known to have happened, \(A\) becomes the new sample space and probabilities are rescaled inside it. Partial information (a negative blood test, a symptom) should revise a probability, usually without driving it to 0 or 1.

2.1.1 · slides 97–100

Conditional probability from a two-way table

To condition on a row or column, divide the cell by that row's or column's total, not the grand total. So \(P(A \mid B)\) and \(P(B \mid A)\) share a numerator but not a denominator: in the loan table \(P(\text{no default} \mid \text{middle-aged}) = 27368/32219 = 0.849\) while \(P(\text{middle-aged} \mid \text{no default}) = 27368/38130 = 0.718\). Swapping them is the base-rate fallacy.

2.1.1, 2.1.3 · slides 102–103, 141, 846–847

Multiplication and chain rules

Rearranging the definition gives \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\), and in general \(P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\,P(C \mid A \cap B)\). This is how sequential draws and tree-diagram branches are multiplied.

2.1.2 · slide 101

Independence through conditioning

\(A\) and \(B\) are independent when \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\): knowing \(A\) does not change the chance of \(B\). For flights, \(P(\text{arrive on time} \mid \text{departed on time}) = 0.75/0.90 = 0.833 \ne 0.80\), so the two events are dependent.

2.1.4 · slides 109, 846–847

With vs without replacement

Drawing with replacement keeps the draws independent, so probabilities multiply unchanged: all 3 of 3 adults right-handed is \((272/300)^3 = 0.745\). Without replacement each draw changes the pool, so the chain rule with shrinking counts is needed; for a large pool and a small sample the two answers are close.

2.1.4 · slides 112–113, 116–117, 142

Law of total probability and tree diagrams

If \(A_1, \dots, A_k\) partition \(S\), \(P(B) = \sum_i P(A_i)\,P(B \mid A_i)\): forests covering 50%, 30%, 20% of a park with 20%, 40%, 70% poisonous plants give \(0.10 + 0.12 + 0.14 = 0.36\). On a tree, multiply along a path for a joint probability and add the paths that end in \(B\) for its total probability.

2.1.5 · slides 118–121, 130–134, 853–855

Simpson's paradox

A comparison that holds in every subgroup can reverse when the subgroups are pooled, because total probability weights each subgroup by its size. Always ask whether a lurking grouping variable has different mixes in the groups being compared.

2.1.5, 2.1.3 · slides 118–121

Bayes' theorem reverses a conditional

\(P(E_i \mid A) = \frac{P(E_i)\,P(A \mid E_i)}{\sum_j P(E_j)\,P(A \mid E_j)}\): the numerator is one tree path and the denominator is the total probability of \(A\). It turns the easy-to-know "probability of the effect given the cause" into the wanted "probability of the cause given the effect".

2.2.1 · slides 148–151, 865

Prior, likelihood, evidence, posterior

The prior \(P(H)\) is the belief before data, the likelihood \(P(E \mid H)\) says how well \(H\) explains the evidence, the evidence \(P(E)\) normalises, and the posterior \(P(H \mid E)\) is the updated belief. Over mutually exclusive, exhaustive hypotheses the posteriors always sum to 1.

2.2.2 · slides 158, 866

Base rates in diagnostic tests and spam filters

A rare condition stays fairly unlikely even after a positive result, because false positives from the large healthy group swamp the true positives. With \(P(\text{spam}) = 0.3\), \(P(\text{offer} \mid \text{spam}) = 0.8\) and \(P(\text{offer} \mid \text{not spam}) = 0.1\), an email containing "offer" is spam with probability \(0.24/0.31 = 0.774\).

2.2.3 · slides 154–157, 159–160

Binary channel and "which machine?"

The same Bayes pattern answers "given a 1 was received, was a 1 sent?" and "given the item is defective, which machine made it?". In the channel with \(P(1) = 0.4\) and correct-reception rates 0.90 and 0.95, \(P(1 \text{ received}) = 0.38 + 0.06 = 0.44\) and \(P(1 \text{ sent} \mid 1 \text{ received}) = 0.38/0.44 = 0.864\).

2.2.3 · slides 124–126, 138–139, 870–876

Odds form and sequential updating

Posterior odds = prior odds \(\times\) likelihood ratio \(P(E \mid H_1)/P(E \mid H_2)\), which shows how strong evidence must be to overcome a small prior. Updating on evidence one piece at a time (yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior) gives the same answer as updating on all of it at once.

2.2.2, 2.2.3 · slides 158, 162

Bayesian learning: MAP vs ML hypothesis

The MAP hypothesis maximises \(P(D \mid h)\,P(h)\); the ML hypothesis maximises \(P(D \mid h)\) alone. They coincide when all hypotheses have equal priors, and a strong prior can make MAP choose a hypothesis that fits the data less well.

2.2.4 · slides 162–163

Conditional independence

\(X\) and \(Y\) are conditionally independent given \(Z\) when \(P(X \mid Y, Z) = P(X \mid Z)\): once lightning is known, rain tells you nothing more about thunder. Conditional independence neither implies nor is implied by ordinary independence.

2.2.5 · slides 170–171, 881

The naive assumption

Naive Bayes assumes the features are conditionally independent given the class, \(P(X_1, \dots, X_n \mid Y) = \prod_i P(X_i \mid Y)\). This is rarely true, but it cuts the number of probabilities to estimate from exponential to linear in the number of features, which is what makes learning from limited data possible.

2.2.5 · slides 167, 171–172, 881–882

Naive Bayes classifier workflow

Build a frequency table per feature and class, turn it into a likelihood table, then score each class by \(P(y)\prod_i P(x_i \mid y)\) and pick the argmax; \(P(X)\) is the same for every class and can be dropped. In the weather data \(P(\text{Yes} \mid \text{Sunny}) = 0.6 > P(\text{No} \mid \text{Sunny}) = 0.4\), so the player plays. Training is just counting, which is MLE.

2.2.6 · slides 164–166, 172–181, 886

Zero-frequency problem and Laplace smoothing

If a feature value never occurs with a class, its estimate is 0 and wipes out the whole product: in the deck every message containing "Lunch" would be called normal however many times "Money" appears. Laplace smoothing adds 1 to every count (and the number of possible values to the denominator), after which "Lunch Money Money Money Money" is classified as spam.

2.2.7 · slides 177–178, 187–190, 885

Naive Bayes variants, strengths and weaknesses

Gaussian NB models each continuous feature per class by a normal density; multinomial NB uses word counts (text); Bernoulli NB uses word presence or absence. NB is fast, needs little data and copes with many features, but its independence assumption is unrealistic, unseen values get zero probability, and its probabilities are poorly calibrated; products of many small numbers are computed as sums of logs to avoid underflow.

M3a · Random Variables & Joint Distributions · 12 items

A random variable is a function

A random variable assigns exactly one real number to each outcome, \(X: S \to \mathbb{R}\) (for two coin tosses, \(X\) = number of heads maps HT to 1). It is discrete if its values can be counted and continuous if it can take any value in an interval; this decides whether you sum a pmf or integrate a pdf.

3.1.1 · slides 220–222, 893–895

Probability mass function

A pmf lists \(P(X = x)\) for each value; it is valid only if every \(p(x) \ge 0\) and \(\sum p(x) = 1\), which is how an unknown \(k\) in a table is found. The discrete uniform pmf gives each of \(n\) values probability \(1/n\), as for a fair die.

3.1.2 · slides 223–226, 897–899

Expectation, linearity and fair games

\(E[X] = \sum x\,p(x)\) is the long-run average (1.75 cars for the deck's drive-thru queue) and need not be a possible value; \(E[g(X)] = \sum g(x)\,p(x)\) and \(E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b\). A game is fair when the expected gain is 0: winning 3 on a dice sum of 7 and losing 2 on an 8 or 4 on a 3 gives \((18 - 10 - 8)/36 = 0\).

3.1.3 · slides 227–228, 230–231

Variance of a random variable

\(\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2\), the second form being easier by hand. Adding a constant does not change spread, while scaling by \(a\) scales the variance by \(a^2\): \(\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\). The drive-thru queue has \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4.05 - 1.75^2 = 0.99\).

3.1.4 · slides 229–231

Cumulative distribution function

\(F(x) = P(X \le x)\) accumulates probability from the left and rises from 0 to 1. For a discrete variable it is a step function whose jumps are the pmf values; for a continuous one it is smooth with \(F'(x) = f(x)\), and \(P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)\).

3.1.5 · slides 232–237

Probability density function

For a continuous variable probability is area: \(f(x) \ge 0\), \(\int f = 1\), and \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). Hence \(P(X = a) = 0\) for every single point, and \(f(a)\) is a density that can exceed 1 (a uniform on \([0, 0.5]\) has \(f = 2\)).

3.1.6 · slides 240–243, 902

Moments of a continuous variable and piecewise densities

Means and variances are found by integration, \(E[X] = \int x f(x)\,dx\). A piecewise density must be integrated piece by piece: for the triangle \(f(x) = x\) on \((0, 1)\) and \(2 - x\) on \((1, 2)\), \(E[X] = 1\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 1/6\) and \(P(0.2 < X < 0.8) = 0.30\).

3.1.6 · slides 244–247, 903–906

Exponential lifetimes and memorylessness

The exponential density \(f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) (\(x \ge 0\)) has \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\) and mean \(1/\lambda\). It is memoryless, \(P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)\): a component that has survived so far is as good as new, which suits waiting times between Poisson events.

3.1.5, 3.1.6 · slides 240–243

Joint distributions and marginals

A joint pmf \(p(x, y)\) or joint pdf \(f(x, y)\) must be non-negative and sum or integrate to 1 over the whole plane. Marginals come from summing or integrating out the other variable, which is why they appear in the margins of a joint table (in the census example \(P(X = 1) = 0.03 + 0.06 + 0.10 = 0.19\)).

3.1.7 · slides 251–256, 907–910

Conditional distributions

\(f(y \mid x) = f(x, y)/f_X(x)\) is the joint distribution sliced at \(X = x\) and renormalised. The conditional mean \(E[Y \mid X = x]\) is the best predictor of \(Y\) from \(X\) under squared error, which is the idea regression later approximates with a line.

3.1.7 · slides 258–261, 912–913

Independence of random variables

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent exactly when \(f(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\) for all \((x, y)\); one failing cell is enough to prove dependence. A joint density on a non-rectangular region (such as a triangle) can never factor, so its variables are always dependent.

3.1.7 · slides 257, 911

From joint distributions to covariance

\(E[XY] = \sum\sum xy\,p(x, y)\) together with \(E[X]\) and \(E[Y]\) gives \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]\). Independence forces covariance 0, but zero covariance does not imply independence (\(Y = X^2\) with \(X\) symmetric about 0).

3.1.7 · slides 267–271, 547
M3b · Standard Distributions, Sampling, CLT & Estimation · 24 items

Bernoulli trial

A single trial with outcome 1 (success, probability \(p\)) or 0 (failure, \(q = 1 - p\)), such as pass/fail or click/no click. Its mean is \(p\) and its variance \(pq\), which is largest at \(p = 0.5\); every count model in the course is built from Bernoulli trials.

3.2.1 · slides 278–282

When a count is binomial

\(X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)\) needs a fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, a constant \(p\) and independent trials. Infections in a room fail independence, and picking 3 of 20 projectors without replacement fails constant \(p\), so neither is binomial.

3.2.2 · slides 283, 285, 292

Binomial probabilities, moments and complements

\(P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x}\) with mean \(np\) and variance \(npq\); if 3 in 5 customers ask for water, exactly 6 of 10 do with probability 0.251. "At least \(k\)" is \(1 - P(X \le k - 1)\) and "less than 9" is \(1 - P(9) - P(10)\): use the side with fewer terms, and read "fewer than" (excludes) and "no more than" (includes) carefully.

3.2.2 · slides 284, 286–291

Inverse binomial problems

Given the moments, solve \(np = \mu\) and \(npq = \sigma^2\): mean 6 and variance 4 give \(q = 2/3\), \(p = 1/3\), \(n = 18\). Given a target, solve for \(n\): to hit a target at least once with probability over \(2/3\) when \(p = 0.2\) needs \(0.8^n < 1/3\), so \(n = 5\) shots.

3.2.2 · slides 337–339

Poisson distribution and rate scaling

The Poisson counts events in a fixed interval when they occur independently at a constant average rate, with \(P(X = x) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x/x!\) and mean = variance = \(\lambda\). Scale \(\lambda\) to the interval asked about: 2 calls per 3 minutes gives \(\lambda = 6\) for 9 minutes, and 5 defects per 10 square feet gives \(\lambda = 7.5\) for 15.

3.2.3 · slides 293–296, 299–301, 331–332

Poisson approximation to the binomial

When \(n\) is large and \(p\) small, \(\text{Bin}(n, p) \approx \text{Poisson}(\lambda = np)\); the deck asks for \(n \ge 100\) and \(np \le 10\). With 5% defective bindings, \(P(2 \text{ of } 100)\) is 0.081 exactly and 0.084 by Poisson with \(\lambda = 5\).

3.2.3 · slides 295, 297–298, 329–330

Normal distribution and the 68–95–99.7 rule

\(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) is bell-shaped and symmetric with mean = median = mode; \(\mu\) shifts it and \(\sigma\) widens it. About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 SDs of the mean whatever \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are, so a value more than 3 SDs out is suspicious.

3.2.4 · slides 303–307

Standardisation and the Z-table

\(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma\) converts any normal to \(N(0, 1)\), so one table serves all problems; symmetry gives \(\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)\). Test scores with \(\mu = 30\), \(\sigma = 4\) give \(P(X < 40) = P(Z < 2.5) = 0.9938\). Since \(aX + b\) of a normal is normal, sums and differences of independent normals are normal too.

3.2.4 · slides 308–316, 333–335

Normal approximation and continuity correction

If \(np \ge 15\) and \(nq \ge 15\) (the course rule; texts also use 10 or 5), \(X \approx N(np, npq)\): 25 people with \(p = 0.36\) give \(np = 9\), so not allowed; 50 students with \(p = 0.31\) give 15.5 and 34.5, so allowed. Because a count is replaced by a curve, widen each boundary by 0.5: \(P(X < 14) = P(X \le 13)\) becomes \(P(Y < 13.5)\).

3.2.5 · slides 317–321, 343

Chi-square distribution

\(\chi^2_k = Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_k^2\) for independent standard normals, so it lives on \([0, \infty)\), is right-skewed and depends only on its degrees of freedom \(k\). It is the sampling distribution of \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2\) and of the chi-square test statistics in M4.

3.2.6 · slides 322–323

Student's t distribution

\(t_k = Z/\sqrt{\chi^2_k/k}\) is bell-shaped and symmetric but has heavier tails than \(N(0, 1)\), because \(s\) is itself estimated; it tends to \(N(0, 1)\) as \(k \to \infty\). Replacing \(\sigma\) by \(s\) in \((\bar{x} - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})\) turns \(z\) into \(t\) with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom.

3.2.6 · slides 324–326

F distribution

\(F(\nu_1, \nu_2) = (\chi^2_{\nu_1}/\nu_1)/(\chi^2_{\nu_2}/\nu_2)\) is the ratio of two scaled chi-squares, right-skewed on \([0, \infty)\), and is used to compare variances and in ANOVA. The order of the degrees of freedom matters: \(F_{1-\alpha}(\nu_1, \nu_2) = 1/F_{\alpha}(\nu_2, \nu_1)\).

3.2.6 · slides 327–328

Population vs sample, parameter vs statistic

A population is every unit of interest (enumerating it is a census) and a sample is the observed subset, used because populations are often infinite or too costly to study. Parameters (\(\mu\), \(\sigma^2\), \(P\), \(\rho\)) are fixed unknown constants; statistics (\(\bar{x}\), \(s^2\), \(\hat{p}\), \(r\)) are computed from the sample and vary from sample to sample.

3.3.1 · slides 349–355, 390

Probability and non-probability sampling

In probability sampling each unit has a known chance of selection: simple random (homogeneous population), systematic (every \(k\)-th unit, \(k = N/n\)), stratified (homogeneous strata sampled in proportion to size) and multistage. Judgement, convenience, quota and snowball samples have no known probability structure, so they cannot support valid inference about the population.

3.3.2 · slides 357–359

Bias, random error and sampling variation

Observed data = truth + bias + random error: bias comes from poor design and does not shrink with \(n\), random error does. Sampling error is the gap between a statistic and its parameter; sampling variation is the statistic changing from sample to sample (the deck's ten samples have means from 1338 to 3035 around 2162). Random selection removes bias and larger samples reduce the variation.

3.3.2, 3.3.3 · slides 356, 360–365

Sampling distribution of the sample mean

The sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) is the distribution of all possible sample means. Its centre is always \(\mu\) (\(E[\bar{x}] = \mu\) for every \(n\)) and its spread is the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), so quadrupling \(n\) halves the SE. Probabilities about \(\bar{x}\) use \(z = (\bar{x} - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})\).

3.3.4 · slides 366–373, 375

Finite population correction

When sampling without replacement from a finite population with \(n/N \ge 0.05\), multiply the SE by \(\sqrt{(N - n)/(N - 1)}\). Sampling 45 of 350 employees (\(n/N = 0.13\)) needs it; as \(N\) grows relative to \(n\) the factor tends to 1 and can be ignored.

3.3.4 · slides 380–382, 437

Central Limit Theorem

For large \(n\) (about 30 or more), \(\bar{x}\) is approximately \(N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)\) whatever the population's shape; only a normal population makes this exact for small \(n\). Vehicle ages with \(\mu = 96\), \(\sigma = 16\) and \(n = 36\) give \(\text{SE} = 2.67\) and \(P(90 < \bar{x} < 100) = 0.921\). The CLT changes the shape and SE with \(n\), never the mean.

3.3.5 · slides 371–379

Sampling distribution of a proportion

The sample proportion \(\hat{p} = x/n\) (30 union members in 100 workers gives 0.30) has mean \(P\) and standard error \(\sqrt{PQ/n}\). By the CLT it is approximately normal when \(nP > 15\) and \(nQ > 15\) in the deck's rule.

3.3.6 · slides 383–387

Point estimation and good estimators

A point estimate is a single statistic used as a guess for a parameter, and it is only as good as the sample is representative. A good estimator is unbiased (centred on the parameter), consistent (converges to it as \(n\) grows), efficient (smallest variance among unbiased rivals) and sufficient (uses all the information in the data about the parameter); \(\bar{x}\) is all four for \(\mu\).

3.3.7 · slides 388–390, 485

Confidence intervals for a mean: z or t

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI is estimate \(\pm\) critical value \(\times\) SE, with 1.96 for 95% and 2.576 for 99%: 85 bills with \(\bar{x} = 510\), \(\sigma = 46\) give \(510 \pm 9.78\). Use \(z\) when \(\sigma\) is known or \(n\) is large, and \(t\) with \(n - 1\) df when \(\sigma\) is unknown in a small sample; the \(t\) interval is wider because it pays for estimating \(\sigma\).

3.3.8 · slides 391–399, 930–932, 935–937

Intervals for a proportion and a difference of means

For a proportion use \(\hat{p} \pm z\sqrt{\hat{p}\hat{q}/n}\). For \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\) use \((\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm z\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}\); if the interval contains 0, the data do not show a difference at that confidence level.

3.3.8 · slides 395, 402–403, 933

Margin of error and sample size

The margin of error is \(E = z\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), so the required size is \(n = (z\sigma/E)^2\), always rounded up. To estimate a mean within 0.5 with \(\sigma = 4\) at 95%, \(n = (1.96 \times 4/0.5)^2 = 245.9\), so 246. Halving \(E\) quadruples \(n\).

3.3.8 · slides 400, 405, 938–939

What "95% confidence" means

The parameter is fixed; the interval is random. "95% confidence" means that the procedure captures \(\mu\) in 95% of repeated samples, not that this particular interval contains \(\mu\) with probability 0.95. Higher confidence gives a wider interval for the same data.

M4 · Hypothesis Testing, MLE & ANOVA · 27 items

Null and alternative hypotheses

\(H_0\) is the hypothesis of no difference (equality, or "any gap is sampling variation"); \(H_1\) is the claim to be established. The equality always sits in \(H_0\), so "the mean is no more than 920" becomes \(H_0: \mu \le 920\) vs \(H_1: \mu > 920\), and the test can only reject or fail to reject \(H_0\).

4.1 · slides 413–417, 940–941

Left, right and two-tailed tests

The direction of \(H_1\) fixes where the rejection region lies: \(<\) puts all of \(\alpha\) in the left tail, \(>\) in the right tail, and \(\ne\) splits it, \(\alpha/2\) in each tail. Words such as "at least", "exceeds" and "differs" decide this, so read them before computing anything.

4.1 · slides 415–416, 421–423, 943

Type I and Type II errors

A Type I error rejects a true \(H_0\) (probability \(\alpha\), the level of significance); a Type II error fails to reject a false \(H_0\) (probability \(\beta\)). For a fixed sample size, lowering \(\alpha\) raises \(\beta\); only more data reduces both. Power \(= 1 - \beta\) is the chance of detecting a real effect.

4.1 · slides 420, 426

Test statistic and critical region

Most test statistics have the form (estimate − hypothesised value)/standard error, measuring the gap in standard errors. Reject \(H_0\) when the statistic falls in the critical region set by \(\alpha\): \(\lvert z \rvert > 1.96\) for a two-tailed 5% test, \(z > 1.645\) right-tailed, and \(\lvert z \rvert > 2.58\) at 1% (not 1.96, as one CS-9 slide misprints).

4.1 · slides 425, 942–943

The p-value and its decision rule

The p-value is the probability, assuming \(H_0\) is true, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed; for a two-tailed test it is twice the tail area. Reject \(H_0\) if \(p \le \alpha\). It is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true, and a large p-value does not prove \(H_0\).

4.1 · slides 420, 424, 944–946

Failing to reject is not accepting

The tyre data give \(z = 2.37\) (printed as 2.30 on the slide): reject at 5% (\(z > 1.96\)) but not at 1% (\(z < 2.58\)). The evidence did not change, only the standard of proof, so "do not reject" means "insufficient evidence", never "\(H_0\) is proved".

4.1 · slides 413, 435, 446

Parametric vs non-parametric; z vs t

Parametric tests assume a distribution with parameters (z, t, F, ANOVA); non-parametric tests do not. Use \(z\) when \(\sigma\) is known or \(n \ge 30\), and \(t\) when \(\sigma\) is unknown and the sample is small from a roughly normal population.

4.1 · slides 418–419, 442, 947–949

Practical significance and multiple testing

With a huge sample a trivial difference becomes "significant", since the SE shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so report an effect size or CI too. Twenty tests of true nulls at \(\alpha = 0.05\) give at least one false positive with probability \(1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.64\); reporting only the winners is p-hacking, and a correction such as Bonferroni (\(\alpha/m\)) is needed.

4.1 · slides 412, 420, 426

z-test for one mean

\(z = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})\), with \(s\) in place of \(\sigma\) for large samples. For 44 sandwiches with \(\bar{x} = 925\) and \(s = 18\) against a claim of at most 920, \(z = 5/2.71 = 1.84 > 1.645\), so the claim is rejected at 5%.

4.2 · slides 425, 427–429, 952–959

z-test for two means

\(z = (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)/\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}\) for independent large samples; a non-zero hypothesised difference \(\mu_1 - \mu_2 = \Delta_0\) is subtracted in the numerator. The tyre example (30 and 40 tyres, SDs 5000 and 6100 km) gives \(z = 3150/1328 = 2.37\), which the slide rounds to 2.30.

4.2 · slides 430–436, 962–965

z-test for one proportion

\(z = (\hat{p} - P_0)/\sqrt{P_0 Q_0/n}\), with the SE built from the hypothesised \(P_0\), not \(\hat{p}\). For 82 type-A donations in 150 against 40%, \(z = (0.547 - 0.40)/0.040 = 3.67\), so the proportion differs.

4.2 · slides 437–438, 968–969

z-test for two proportions (pooled)

Under \(H_0: P_1 = P_2\) both samples estimate one common \(P\), so pool them: \(\hat{p} = (x_1 + x_2)/(n_1 + n_2)\) and \(z = (\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2)/\sqrt{\hat{p}\hat{q}(1/n_1 + 1/n_2)}\). The judges example gives \(z = 2.55 > 1.645\); when testing a non-zero difference, use the unpooled SE instead.

4.2 · slides 439–441, 972–973

One-sample t-test

\(t = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})\) with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom, assuming independent observations from a roughly normal population. Ten sports cars give \(t = 1.929\): reject at 5% (\(t_{0.05, 9} = 1.833\)) but not at 1% (2.821).

4.3 · slides 442–447, 956–961

Unpaired (pooled) t-test

For two independent small samples with equal population variances, pool \(s_p^2 = [(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2]/(n_1 + n_2 - 2)\) and use \(t = (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)/(s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2})\) with \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\) df. The courier packets give \(t = 2.233 > 2.069\) (df 23).

4.3 · slides 448–449, 966–967, 476

Paired t-test

When each subject is measured twice (before/after), analyse the differences \(d\) as one sample: \(t = \bar{d}/(s_d/\sqrt{n})\) with \(n - 1\) df. Pairing removes subject-to-subject variation, so it detects effects an unpaired test would miss; the training example gives \(t = 1.629 < 1.833\), not significant.

4.3 · slides 450–452, 474–475, 477

Duality of intervals and tests

A two-tailed test at level \(\alpha\) rejects \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) exactly when \(\mu_0\) lies outside the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI. The courier 95% CI for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\) is (0.073, 1.907), which excludes 0, matching the rejection.

4.3 · slides 449, 473

Chi-square test of independence

Compare observed counts with the counts expected if the two categorical variables were unrelated, \(E_{ij} = (\text{row total})(\text{column total})/n\), using \(\chi^2 = \sum (O - E)^2/E\) with \((r-1)(c-1)\) df. The accident timings table gives \(\chi^2 = 7.32 > 3.84\) (df 1), so timing and outcome are associated.

4.4 · slides 453–466, 479

Chi-square goodness of fit

Tests whether counts follow a hypothesised distribution, with df \(= k - 1 - m\) for \(k\) categories and \(m\) parameters estimated from the data. Telephone digits give \(\chi^2 = 58.54 > 16.92\) (df 9), so they are not uniform; a Poisson fit with \(\lambda\) estimated from 6 categories has df \(= 6 - 1 - 1 = 4\).

4.4 · slides 467–471

Conditions for chi-square tests

Use raw frequencies only, never percentages or means, and require every expected count to be at least 5; otherwise pool adjacent categories. In the deck's \(2 \times 2\) hospital-stay table one expected count is \(25 \times 10/70 = 3.6\), so the test should not be applied as it stands. For a \(2 \times 2\) table, \(\chi^2\) equals the square of the two-proportion \(z\).

4.4 · slides 454, 478

Likelihood and maximum likelihood

A likelihood fixes the observed data and treats \(L(\theta) = \prod f(x_i \mid \theta)\) as a function of the parameter; the MLE is the value that makes the data most probable. For 3 flawed helmets out of 10, \(L(0.25) = 0.0021\) beats \(L(0.5) = 0.00098\) and the peak is at \(p = 0.3\). Maximise \(\ln L\) instead (same maximiser, a sum not a product) by setting its derivative to 0.

4.5 · slides 484–495

Standard MLEs and MLE as a loss

Bernoulli/binomial \(\hat{p} = k/n\), Poisson \(\hat{\lambda} = \bar{x}\), normal \(\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = SS/n\), which is biased (too small) yet still the MLE. Maximising a normal likelihood of residuals is least squares, and maximising a Bernoulli likelihood is minimising cross-entropy, so most loss functions are MLE in disguise.

4.5 · slides 487–492, 496–500

Invariance of the MLE

The MLE of any function \(g(\theta)\) is \(g(\hat{\theta})\). Defective-drive counts 7, 3, 1, 2, 4, 1, 2, 3, 1, 2 give \(\hat{\lambda} = 2.6\), so the MLE of \(P(0 \text{ or } 1 \text{ defectives})\) is \(e^{-2.6}(1 + 2.6) = 0.267\).

4.5 · slide 529

F-test for two variances

To test \(H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\) for two independent normal samples, use \(F = s_1^2/s_2^2\) with the larger sample variance on top, so only the upper tail is needed, and df \((n_1 - 1, n_2 - 1)\) in that order. It checks the equal-variance assumption behind the pooled t-test and ANOVA.

4.6 · slides 501–503, 328

ANOVA: comparing several means through variances

ANOVA tests \(H_0: \mu_1 = \dots = \mu_k\) against "not all means are equal" (not "\(\mu_1 \ne \mu_2 \ne \mu_3\)"). It needs normal responses, equal variances across groups and independent observations; running many pairwise t-tests instead would inflate the Type I error.

4.7 · slides 504–505

One-way ANOVA and the correction factor

\(SST = SSTR + SSE\) (between plus within groups) with df \(n - 1 = (k - 1) + (n - k)\), and \(F = MSTR/MSE\) is large when group means differ more than noise explains; the deck's groups with means 10, 8, 12 give \(F = 20/5 = 4 > 3.89\). By hand use \(CF = G^2/n\), \(SST = \sum x^2 - CF\), \(SSTR = \sum C_j^2/n_j - CF\); the glue data give \(F = 3.08 < 3.89\) (p = 0.084).

4.7 · slides 506–516, 533–534

Two-way ANOVA without replication

Controlling a second factor (blocks) removes its variation from the error: \(SST = SSTR + SSBL + SSE\), error df \((k-1)(m-1)\), with separate F-ratios for treatments and blocks (the fertiliser data give 3.6 and 2.4, both below 4.76 and 5.14). With several observations per cell, use two-way ANOVA with replication to test interaction; analysing cell means, as the teaching-method slide does, discards the replicates.

4.7 · slides 517–528

Post-hoc comparisons

Rejecting \(H_0\) in ANOVA says only that at least one pair of means differs. A post-hoc test such as Fisher's LSD, \(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-k}\sqrt{MSE(1/n_i + 1/n_j)}\), then compares each pair; a pair differs if its mean gap exceeds the LSD. With two groups, ANOVA's \(F\) equals the pooled \(t^2\).

M5 · Prediction & Forecasting I — Correlation & Regression · 19 items

Covariance gives direction only

Sample covariance \(\sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y})/(n - 1)\) is positive when the variables rise together and negative when one falls as the other rises. Its size depends on the units, \(\operatorname{Cov}(aX + b, cY + d) = ac\,\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)\) (age and weight give 10.6 "years·kg"), so it cannot measure strength.

5.1 · slides 541–543, 545–546

Covariance from a distribution

For random variables, \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]\), computed from a joint pmf or pdf. It links the joint distributions of M3 to correlation, and \(\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)\).

5.1 · slide 547

Variance–covariance matrix

For \(n\) variables, \(\Sigma\) holds variances on the diagonal and covariances off it, and is symmetric (\(\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji}\)). It summarises all pairwise linear relations and is the input to multivariate regression, PCA and multivariate Gaussians (GMM).

5.1 · slides 544, 548

PCA as diagonalising the covariance matrix

PCA finds the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\): new variables (principal components) that are uncorrelated linear combinations of the originals. Each eigenvalue is the variance along its component, so large eigenvalues mark important directions and near-zero ones can be dropped, reducing dimension.

5.1 · slides 550–551

Autocovariance

Applying covariance to a series and its own lagged copy gives the autocovariance at lag \(h\), and dividing by the variance gives the autocorrelation. This is the bridge from M5 to the stationarity and ARMA ideas of M6.

5.1 · slides 548–549

Pearson's r: standardised covariance

\(r = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/(s_X s_Y)\) is unit-free and lies in \([-1, 1]\); its sign is the direction and its size the strength of the linear relationship (children's ages and weights give \(r = 0.759\), anxiety and test score \(r = -0.94\)). It is unchanged by shifting or positively rescaling either variable, so it can be computed from coded values.

5.2 · slides 552–562, 982–988

Strength bands and scatter diagrams

The deck grades \(\lvert r \rvert\) as weak up to 0.25, moderate from 0.26 to 0.75 and strong from 0.76 to 0.99, with \(\pm 1\) perfect. Always draw the scatter diagram first: it shows curvature, clusters and outliers that a single number hides.

5.2 · slides 557, 563–573, 980–981

Reading r with care

\(r\) measures only linear association: \(y = x^2\) on a symmetric range has \(r = 0\) despite perfect dependence, and one extreme point can manufacture a large \(r\). A strong \(r\) can also come from a lurking variable driving both (hot weather drives ice-cream sales and drownings), so correlation is not causation.

5.2 · slides 553–555, 567–569

Spearman's rank correlation

Rank each variable and compute \(R = 1 - 6\sum d^2/(n(n^2 - 1))\) from the rank differences \(d\). It is Pearson's \(r\) applied to ranks, so it measures monotone rather than linear association, suits ordinal data or non-normal populations, and resists outliers; the deck's first example gives \(R = 0.14\).

5.3 · slides 989–992

Tied ranks

Tied values share the average of the ranks they occupy (two values tied for 3rd and 4th both get 3.5). Ties reduce rank variance, so add \(m(m^2 - 1)/12\) to \(\sum d^2\) for every tie group of size \(m\), in both variables, before applying the formula.

5.3 · slides 990, 993–994

Simple linear regression model

\(Y = a + bX + \varepsilon\): \(Y\) is the response, \(X\) the predictor, \(b\) the change in mean \(Y\) per unit of \(X\), and \(\varepsilon\) the random error. The errors are assumed to have mean 0, constant variance \(\sigma^2\), independence and a normal distribution.

5.4 · slides 574–577

Least squares and the normal equations

Choose \(a\) and \(b\) to minimise \(\sum (Y - a - bX)^2\); setting both partial derivatives to zero gives the normal equations \(\sum Y = na + b\sum X\) and \(\sum XY = a\sum X + b\sum X^2\). Their solution is \(b = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\), \(a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}\), so the line passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) and the residuals sum to 0.

5.4 · slides 578–581, 1007–1010

Prediction and extrapolation

For the cinema data \(\hat{Y} = 92.45 + 0.407X\), so 4.5 thousand of TV advertising predicts revenue of 94.28 thousand. Predictions are trustworthy only inside the range of observed \(X\); extrapolating assumes the straight line continues where no data support it.

5.4 · slides 582–584, 1001

Two regression lines

The line of \(Y\) on \(X\) (minimising vertical errors) and the line of \(X\) on \(Y\) (horizontal errors) differ unless \(\lvert r \rvert = 1\), and both pass through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\), so solving the two equations gives the means (\(\bar{x} = 13\), \(\bar{y} = 17\) in the deck). Use \(Y\) on \(X\) to predict \(Y\) and \(X\) on \(Y\) to predict \(X\).

5.4 · slides 995–1006

Properties of the regression coefficients

\(b_{yx} = r\,\sigma_y/\sigma_x\) and \(b_{xy} = r\,\sigma_x/\sigma_y\), so \(r^2 = b_{yx}b_{xy}\): \(r\) is the geometric mean of the two coefficients and shares their sign. Both coefficients cannot exceed 1 in absolute value, since their product is at most 1.

5.4 · slides 984, 996, 1000

Decomposing variation: SST = SSR + SSE

Total variation about \(\bar{y}\) splits into the part explained by the line (\(SSR = \sum (\hat{y} - \bar{y})^2\)) and the unexplained residual part (\(SSE = \sum (y - \hat{y})^2\)). The cross-term vanishes because least-squares residuals are uncorrelated with the fitted values.

5.5 · slides 585–589

Coefficient of determination

\(r^2 = SSR/SST\) is the share of the variation in \(Y\) explained by the line, equal to the square of Pearson's \(r\) in simple regression: TV adverts explain 87.7% of the variation in cars sold. A high \(r^2\) does not prove the form is right (Anscombe's data share one \(r^2\)); a curved residual plot calls for curve fitting.

5.5 · slides 590–594

Fitting a parabola

\(y = a + bx + cx^2\) is still linear in \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), so least squares gives three normal equations built from \(\sum x, \sum x^2, \sum x^3, \sum x^4, \sum y, \sum xy, \sum x^2 y\). Coding \(x\) to centre it at 0 kills the odd sums and makes the system easy by hand.

5.6 · slides 1011–1014

Linearising power and exponential curves

Take logs to turn a curve into a straight line: \(y = ax^b\) gives \(\log y = \log a + b \log x\), and \(y = ae^{bx}\) or \(y = ab^x\) gives \(\log y\) linear in \(x\). Fit the line by least squares, then back-transform the intercept (\(a = 10^{A}\)); the fit then minimises errors in \(\log y\), not in \(y\).

M6 · Prediction & Forecasting II — Time Series; GMM & EM · 34 items

Time series vs cross-sectional data

A time series is a sequence \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n\) of one variable at equally spaced times, and its order is the information. Marks against study hours for many students at one time is cross-sectional (explain \(Y\) by \(X\), as in M5); admissions per year is a time series (explain and forecast \(Y_t\) from its own past).

6.1 · slides 599–605, 1022–1023

Trend, seasonal, cyclical and irregular components

Trend \(T\) is the long-run direction; seasonality \(S\) repeats with a fixed, known period of a year or less (months, quarters, weekdays); cycles \(C\) are recurring but non-periodic swings longer than a year (business cycles); the irregular part \(I\) is unpredictable noise left after removing the others. Fixed period separates seasonal from cyclical.

6.1 · slides 606–618

Additive vs multiplicative decomposition

Additive \(Y = T + S + C + I\) suits seasonal swings of constant size; multiplicative \(Y = T \times S \times C \times I\) suits swings that grow in proportion to the level (seasonal indices such as 1.2 mean "20% above trend"). Taking logs turns a multiplicative model into an additive one.

6.1 · slides 619–621, 715

Forecasting vs planning; horizons

Forecasting estimates future values from past patterns with a stated method, prediction is broader and may rest on judgement; a forecast says what is likely, a goal what is wanted, and planning closes the gap. Short-term forecasts drive scheduling, medium-term ones budgets and resources, long-term ones strategy, where the trend dominates.

6.1 · slides 622–628

Qualitative vs quantitative methods

Qualitative methods (personal judgement, panel consensus, Delphi, market research) are used when data are scarce or the future breaks from the past. Quantitative methods are either time-series methods (smoothing, exponential smoothing, trend projection) or causal methods (regression on explanatory variables).

6.1 · slides 629, 1024

Simple moving average

A \(k\)-period MA averages \(k\) neighbouring values to smooth out irregular and seasonal ups and downs (production 21, 22, 23, 25, 24 gives 22.00, 23.33, 24.00); a trailing MA of the last \(k\) values is the next forecast. It is a low-pass filter that lags a trend by about \((k - 1)/2\) periods, so longer windows are smoother but slower and \(k\) is chosen by forecast error.

6.2 · slides 630, 635–638, 709–711, 1025–1028

Centred moving average for even \(k\)

An even-length MA falls between two time points, so a second 2-period average of the MAs (a \(2 \times k\) MA) recentres it. A \(2 \times 4\) MA on quarterly data, or \(2 \times 12\) on monthly data, removes the seasonal pattern exactly.

6.2 · slides 631, 639

Weighted moving average

Weights that sum to 1, such as 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 from newest to oldest, let recent data count more, so the forecast reacts faster to change than a simple MA of the same length.

6.2 · slides 640, 643

Least-squares trend with coded time

Fit \(Y = a + bX\) by least squares with \(X\) coded so that \(\sum X = 0\) (for 7 years, \(X = t - \text{middle year}\)); then \(a = \sum Y/n\) and \(b = \sum XY/\sum X^2\) directly. To forecast a future year, convert it to the same code before substituting.

6.2 · slides 1034–1038

Forecast errors and accuracy measures

With \(e_t = Y_t - F_t\), raw errors cancel and only show bias. MSE punishes large misses most, MAD treats errors linearly in the data's units, MAPE is scale-free but breaks when actuals are near 0, and LAD reports the worst miss; errors of size 2, 3, 3, 4, 4 give MSE 10.8, MAD 3.2 and LAD 4. Choose the measure that matches the cost of being wrong.

6.3 · slides 632–634, 701–702, 709–713

Simple exponential smoothing

\(F_{t+1} = \alpha Y_t + (1 - \alpha)F_t\), started with \(F_2 = Y_1\), unrolls to \(\alpha Y_t + \alpha(1 - \alpha)Y_{t-1} + \alpha(1 - \alpha)^2 Y_{t-2} + \dots\): all past values with geometrically shrinking weights. Monthly sales 50, 55, 52, 58, 60 with \(\alpha = 0.3\) give a June forecast of 55.49; SES suits series with no trend or seasonality.

6.4 · slides 696–697, 703, 716, 744–749

The smoothing constant \(\alpha\)

\(\alpha = 1\) gives the naive forecast \(F_{t+1} = Y_t\); \(\alpha = 0\) freezes the forecast at its starting value. Small \(\alpha\) is smooth but slow, large \(\alpha\) is responsive but noisy, so \(\alpha\) is a bias–variance trade-off chosen by comparing MSE for candidates such as 0.2, 0.4 and 0.7, ideally out of sample.

6.4 · slides 696, 703–708

Holt's double exponential smoothing

On a trend SES lags by \(b(1 - \alpha)/\alpha\), so Holt also smooths a slope: \(L_t = \alpha Y_t + (1 - \alpha)(L_{t-1} + T_{t-1})\), \(T_t = \beta(L_t - L_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta)T_{t-1}\) and \(F_{t+k} = L_t + kT_t\). On perfectly linear sales 100, 110, …, 140 it tracks \(L_t = Y_t\), \(T_t = 10\) and forecasts 150.

6.4 · slides 716–729, 741–743

Holt–Winters triple smoothing

Adds a seasonal index \(S_t\) with period \(M\) (4 for quarters, 12 for months): the level uses the deseasonalised value \(Y_t/S_{t-M}\), the trend update is \(T_t = \beta(L_t - L_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta)T_{t-1}\) (correcting the slide's misprint), and the forecast is \((L_t + T_t)S_{t-M+1}\). Initial indices are each season's value divided by the first year's average.

6.4 · slides 730–740

Taxonomy of exponential smoothing

Methods are classified by trend (none, linear/additive, ratio/multiplicative) crossed with seasonality (none, linear, ratio), giving nine cases. SES is no trend and no seasonality, Holt is linear trend and no seasonality, and multiplicative Holt–Winters is linear trend with ratio seasonality.

6.4 · slides 750–759

Strict vs weak stationarity

A strictly stationary series has a joint distribution unchanged by shifts in time; a weakly stationary one only needs a constant mean, constant variance and an autocovariance \(\gamma(h)\) that depends on the lag \(h\) alone. Trends, seasonality and random walks all violate it.

6.5 · slides 649–653

Autocovariance and the ACF

\(\gamma(h) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t+h})\) and \(r_k = \gamma(k)/\gamma(0)\) measure how strongly a series is related to its own past at lag \(k\). A slowly decaying ACF signals a trend or unit root; spikes at lags 4 or 12 signal seasonality.

6.5 · slides 651–660

White noise

White noise is a sequence of uncorrelated values with mean 0 and constant variance, so its true ACF is 0 at every non-zero lag (Gaussian white noise is also normal). Sample autocorrelations within about \(\pm 2/\sqrt{n}\) are consistent with it, and a good model's residuals should look like white noise.

6.5 · slides 662–665

Partial autocorrelation (PACF)

The PACF at lag \(k\) is the correlation between \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-k}\) after removing the effect of the lags in between. If \(\rho_1 = 0.8\) and \(\rho_2 = 0.64 = \rho_1^2\), the lag-2 PACF is 0: the day before yesterday matters only through yesterday.

6.5 · slides 668–669

Dickey–Fuller unit-root test

Rewrite \(Y_t = \phi Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) as \(\Delta Y_t = \delta Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with \(\delta = \phi - 1\), and test \(H_0: \phi = 1\) (\(\delta = 0\), unit root, non-stationary) against \(H_1: \phi < 1\) (stationary). The statistic \(\hat{\delta}/\text{SE}(\hat{\delta})\) is compared with Dickey–Fuller critical values (about \(-2.89\) at 5% with a constant), which are more negative than Student-t ones.

6.5 · slides 677–682

Differencing

The first difference \(\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}\) removes a linear trend and turns a random walk into white noise; a seasonal difference \(Y_t - Y_{t-12}\) removes yearly seasonality. Difference only as many times as needed, since over-differencing adds spurious correlation.

6.5 · slides 688, 765–766

Autoregressive models AR(p)

\(y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t\) regresses a stationary series on its own past, and its coefficients can be fitted by regressing \(y_t\) on lagged copies of itself. Its ACF decays gradually while its PACF cuts off after lag \(p\), which identifies \(p\).

6.6 · slides 670–676, 761

Moving-average models MA(q) and ARMA(p, q)

MA(q) writes \(y_t\) as a mean plus the current shock and \(q\) past shocks \(\theta_j \varepsilon_{t-j}\); its ACF cuts off after lag \(q\) (MA(1) has \(\rho_1 = \theta/(1 + \theta^2)\)). ARMA(p, q) combines both to capture richer autocorrelation with few parameters; both its ACF and PACF tail off.

6.6 · slides 683–687, 763

Recursive forecasting and the long-run mean

Forecast step by step, replacing unknown future shocks by their mean 0. For \(y_t = 2 + 0.7y_{t-1}\) with \(y_{100} = 50\): 37, then 27.9, then 21.53, decaying toward \(c/(1 - \phi) = 6.67\). In ARMA(1,1) the MA term affects only the next forecast (8.8, then 7.4).

6.6 · slides 762, 764

ARIMA(p, d, q)

Difference the series \(d\) times until it is stationary, fit ARMA(p, q) to the differences, then integrate back by cumulative summing. In the deck, \(w_t = 0.4w_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t + 0.3\varepsilon_{t-1}\) with \(y_{100} = 120\) and \(w_{100} = 5\) gives \(\hat{w}_{101} = 2.3\), so \(\hat{y}_{101} = 122.3\) and \(\hat{y}_{102} = 123.22\).

6.6 · slides 688–692, 765–766

SARIMA, SARIMAX, VAR and VARMAX

SARIMA \((p, d, q)(P, D, Q)_m\) adds seasonal AR, MA and differencing at lag \(m\) (\(y_t = 5 + 0.8y_{t-12}\) forecasts 165 from last January's 200). SARIMAX adds exogenous regressors (a promotion flag with \(\beta = 8\) lifts 34 to 42). VAR models several series on each other's lags, and VARMAX adds MA terms and exogenous inputs.

6.6 · slides 767–775

Validating time-series models

Never shuffle a time series into random train/test splits: future values would leak into training and errors would look too good. Hold out the most recent periods, or use a rolling origin, and check that residuals are white noise.

6.3, 6.6 · slides 701, 709

Gaussian mixture model

A GMM models a density as a weighted sum of \(K\) Gaussians, \(p(x) = \sum_k \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)\), with mixing weights \(\pi_k \ge 0\) summing to 1. Mixing 1000 share prices around Rs. 90 with 800 around Rs. 100 gives a two-peaked density that no single Gaussian fits.

6.7 · slides 779–785, 793–794, 798

Soft vs hard clustering

GMM gives every point a probability of belonging to each component (soft clustering, clusters may overlap), whereas k-means assigns each point to exactly one cluster (hard). Assigning a point to its most probable component is a separate decision taken after fitting.

6.7 · slides 780, 791

Latent variables and mixing coefficients as priors

Each point has a hidden 1-of-K indicator \(z\) saying which component generated it, with \(p(z_k = 1) = \pi_k\). So \(\pi_k\) is the prior probability of component \(k\), and \(p(x)\) is obtained by summing \(p(z)p(x \mid z)\) over the hidden states.

6.7 · slides 796–799

Responsibilities are Bayes' theorem

The responsibility \(\gamma(z_{nk}) = \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_n \mid k)/\sum_j \pi_j \mathcal{N}(x_n \mid j)\) is the posterior probability that component \(k\) produced \(x_n\). With \(\pi = (0.6, 0.4)\), means 90 and 100 and equal \(\sigma = 5\), the point \(x = 95\) is equidistant, the densities cancel and its responsibility is just the prior, 0.6.

6.7 · slides 800, 811–813

Why GMM needs EM

The log-likelihood \(\sum_n \ln \sum_k \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_n \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)\) has a sum inside the log, so setting derivatives to zero gives coupled equations with no closed form. If the labels were known each component could be fitted separately, and EM exploits exactly that.

6.7 · slides 797, 801–805

The EM algorithm

Initialise; E-step: compute responsibilities; M-step: \(N_k = \sum_n \gamma_{nk}\), \(\mu_k = \sum_n \gamma_{nk} x_n/N_k\), \(\pi_k = N_k/N\); repeat until the log-likelihood stops changing. For \(x = \{92, 95, 98\}\), responsibilities 0.83, 0.60, 0.31 give \(\pi_1 = 0.58\) and \(\mu_1 = 94.1\). Each cycle never lowers the likelihood, but it reaches only a local maximum, so several starts are used.

6.7 · slides 787–789, 804–809, 814–817

GMM issues and uses

A component that collapses onto one point (\(\sigma \to 0\)) sends the likelihood to infinity (a singularity), \(K\) must be chosen (for example by BIC, \(-2\ln L + p\ln n\)), and the Gaussian assumption may not fit. GMMs are used for soft clustering, density estimation and anomaly detection, where a point with very low \(p(x)\) is flagged; with equal shared variances shrinking to 0 the responsibilities become hard and EM becomes k-means.

2.3 The most common misconceptions (targeted by L1 and L5 problems)

Misconception Reality Targeted in
"Mutually exclusive means independent." If \(P(A), P(B) > 0\), mutually exclusive events are always dependent. M1
"\(P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A)\)." They differ unless \(P(A) = P(B)\). This is the base-rate fallacy. M2
"A 95% CI contains \(\mu\) with probability 0.95." \(\mu\) is fixed. The procedure captures \(\mu\) in 95% of repeated samples. M3
"Not rejecting \(H_0\) proves \(H_0\)." It only means the evidence is insufficient. M4
"\(p\text{-value} = P(H_0 \text{ true})\)." \(p\text{-value} = P(\text{data at least this extreme} \mid H_0)\). M4
"\(r = 0\) means no relationship." It means no linear relationship. \(y = x^2\) on a symmetric range gives \(r = 0\). M5
"Correlation implies causation." Lurking variables can produce correlation without causation. M5
"Sample variance should divide by \(n\)." Dividing by \(n\) gives a biased estimate. \(n - 1\) is unbiased. The MLE divides by \(n\), and that is fine for an MLE. M1, M4
"Larger \(\alpha\) in SES is always better." \(\alpha\) is a bias–variance trade-off. It should be tuned by out-of-sample error. M6
"GMM gives each point one cluster." GMM gives soft responsibilities. Assigning points to clusters is a separate decision step. M6
"\(P(X = a)\) for a continuous RV is \(f(a)\)." \(P(X = a) = 0\). \(f(a)\) is a density and can exceed 1. M3
"Mean of the CLT sampling distribution changes with \(n\)." \(E[\bar{x}] = \mu\) for every \(n\). Only the SE (and the shape) change. M3

2.5 How the problem bank is designed

  • Every leaf of the tree is hit by at least one problem. Bottleneck nodes (\(Z\) and SE, conditioning, tails) are hit 4–8 times at increasing levels.
  • L1 problems check vocabulary and catch misconceptions before computation starts.
  • L2 problems use small, clean numbers, so mechanics are separated from reading comprehension.
  • L3 problems mirror the style of the BITS EC-2 and EC-3 exams that appear in the webinars.
  • L4 problems make you derive the formulas: why \(n - 1\), where the normal equations come from, why Poisson's mean equals its variance, why SST splits. Once you can derive a formula, you no longer need to memorise it.
  • L5 problems link the topic to ML practice (Naive Bayes, PCA, model diagnostics, forecasting, clustering) or set deliberate traps.
  • Each module ends with a capstone that uses the whole module. Part 10 holds cross-module capstones.

Suggested study loop per topic: do all L1 \(\to\) all L2 \(\to\) L3 \(\to\) L4, and check each answer as you go. Then do L5 without hints. Re-attempt any problem you got wrong 48 hours later.
