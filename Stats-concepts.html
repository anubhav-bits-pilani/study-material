CS-1 — Descriptive Statistics · source: S1-26_ISM_CS-1_PPT.pdf

What Statistics is

A branch of applied mathematics for collecting, organising, analysing, and inferring from data, then taking data-driven decisions. It is the backbone of Data Science, AI/ML and information technologies.

ISM CS-1 · p7–8

Types of data

Data is numerical (quantitative) or categorical (qualitative). Quantitative splits into discrete (countable, e.g. number of children) and continuous (measured, e.g. weight, voltage). Categorical examples: marital status, eye colour.

ISM CS-1 · p9,11

Levels of measurement

Nominal (categories, no order) → Ordinal (ordered categories) → Interval (meaningful differences, no true zero, e.g. °C) → Ratio (true zero, ratios meaningful, e.g. weight, age). Choosing the right level dictates which statistics are valid.

ISM CS-1 · p10–12

Measures of central tendency

A single value summarising the "centre" of a data set: mean (arithmetic average), median (middle value), mode (most frequent). They reduce a whole distribution to one representative number.

ISM CS-1 · p13

Mean — when to use

Most stable measure since every score contributes, but easily distorted by outliers. Best for quantitative interval/ratio data without extreme values, and when SD/CV/skewness are to be computed. The sum of deviations from the mean is always zero.

ISM CS-1 · p14,17

Median — when to use

The 50th percentile; 50% of values fall below it. Never affected by outliers, so it is the preferred centre for heavily skewed data and ordinal scales.

ISM CS-1 · p20–22

Mode — when to use

The value with the largest frequency. Works for qualitative and quantitative data, is not affected by extremes, but may not be unique. Best on nominal scales or when the most-frequent outcome drives a decision.

ISM CS-1 · p18–19

Skewness & symmetry

Symmetric: mean = median = mode. Right (positive) skew: mean > median > mode, long right tail. Left (negative) skew: mean < median < mode, long left tail. Distributions may be bimodal or multimodal.

ISM CS-1 · p23

Why the mean alone is not enough

Two very different data sets can share the same mean, median and mode; only a measure of spread distinguishes them. This motivates measures of variability.

ISM CS-1 · p24–27

Measures of variability

Describe how scattered scores are and how well the centre represents the data: range, variance, standard deviation, and quartile deviation. Range uses only two values so is imprecise; SD/variance use all data.

ISM CS-1 · p27–30

Variance & standard deviation

Because raw deviations from the mean sum to zero, we square them, sum (SS), and average → variance; its square root is the SD, the average distance from the mean. SD describes whether scores cluster near or scatter from the mean.

ISM CS-1 · p31–35

Population vs sample (the n−1 correction)

A sample has less variability than its population, so dividing SS by \(n\) underestimates the true variance. Dividing by \(n-1\) (Bessel's correction) removes this bias; notation switches from \(\sigma\) to \(s\).

ISM CS-1 · p38–39

Five-number summary

Minimum, Q1 (25%), median (Q2), Q3 (75%), maximum — a compact description of location and spread used to build box plots.

ISM CS-1 · p43

Interquartile range & quartile deviation

IQR = Q3 − Q1 measures the spread of the middle 50% and is not affected by extremes; QD = IQR/2. Robust alternatives to range and SD for skewed data.

ISM CS-1 · p44

Box-and-whisker plot

Visualises the five-number summary; whiskers reach to Q1−1.5·IQR and Q3+1.5·IQR. Points beyond are outliers; points beyond Q1−3·IQR or Q3+3·IQR are major (extreme) outliers.

ISM CS-1 · p45–46

Outlier detection (1.5·IQR rule)

Lower limit = Q1 − 1.5·IQR, upper limit = Q3 + 1.5·IQR. Values outside these fences are potential outliers; data entirely inside is free of outliers.

CS-2 — Probability Basics: Axioms, Events, Independence · source: S1-26_ISM_CS-2_PPT.pdf

Random experiment

Any action whose outcome is not known in advance (coin flip, drawing a card, waiting time for a bus). Probability studies such uncertain experiments.

ISM CS-2 · p4–5

Sample space and events

The sample space \(S\) is the set of all possible outcomes; an event is any subset of \(S\). An event "occurs" if the realised outcome belongs to it. The empty set and the whole \(S\) are both events.

ISM CS-2 · p6–7

Operations on events

Complement \(A^c\): all outcomes not in \(A\). Union \(A\cup B\): outcomes in \(A\) or \(B\) or both. Intersection \(A\cap B\): outcomes in both. Basis for combining probabilities.

ISM CS-2 · p8–9

Mutually exclusive events

Two events with no common outcomes — if one occurs the other cannot (e.g. a single coin toss landing heads vs tails). Then \(P(A\cap B)=0\).

ISM CS-2 · p10

Independent vs dependent events

Independent: one event's occurrence gives no information about the other. Dependent: it does. Independent events can occur simultaneously, so independence is not the same as mutual exclusivity — in fact two events with non-zero probability that are mutually exclusive must be dependent.

ISM CS-2 · p17–18; Webinar 1 · s23

Three approaches to probability

Classical (equally likely outcomes: favourable / total), empirical (long-run relative frequency from data), and axiomatic (defined by Kolmogorov's axioms). Probability is a number in [0,1] measuring likelihood, with 0.5 meaning equally likely as unlikely.

ISM CS-2 · p11–14

Addition rule

\(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\); for mutually exclusive events the last term is zero. Used for "A or B" questions and to recover intersection probabilities.

ISM CS-2 · p15

Permissible probability assignments

An assignment is valid only if every probability lies in [0,1] and (for a full set of mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes) they sum to 1. Negative values or totals ≠ 1 are impermissible.

ISM CS-2 · p19–20

Counting: permutations & combinations

Combinations \(\binom{n}{r}\) count unordered selections and appear when computing classical probabilities (e.g. choosing a committee, selecting motors). Order matters → permutations; order does not → combinations.

ISM CS-2 · p33,36
CS-3 — Conditional & Total Probability · source: S1-26_ISM_CS-3_PPT.pdf

Conditional probability

The probability of \(B\) given that \(A\) has occurred, \(P(B\mid A)=P(A\cap B)/P(A)\). New (partial) information about the experiment revises earlier probability assignments — e.g. a disease probability changes after a test result.

ISM CS-3 · p4–6

Multiplication rule

\(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)\); for three events \(P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A)P(B\mid A)P(C\mid A\cap B)\). Used to chain sequential dependent events (e.g. drawing cards without replacement).

ISM CS-3 · p8

Independence test

Events are independent iff \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\), equivalently \(P(B\mid A)=P(B)\). Comparing the observed joint probability against the product of marginals decides independence.

ISM CS-3 · p16; ISM CS-2 · p32

With vs without replacement

With replacement the trials are independent (constant probabilities); without replacement each draw changes the remaining pool, so conditional probabilities and the multiplication rule apply.

ISM CS-3 · p19–20,23

Total probability theorem

If \(A_1,\dots,A_k\) partition \(S\) (mutually exclusive and exhaustive), then \(P(B)=\sum_i P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)\). It combines branch probabilities in a tree diagram to get an overall (unconditional) probability.

ISM CS-3 · p25–28,40
CS-4 — Bayes' Theorem & Naïve Bayes · source: S1-26_ISM_CS-4_PPT.pdf

Bayes' theorem

Inverts conditional probabilities: given a partition \(E_1,\dots,E_n\), \(P(E_i\mid A)=\dfrac{P(E_i)P(A\mid E_i)}{\sum_j P(E_j)P(A\mid E_j)}\). Lets us update a hypothesis's probability after observing evidence.

ISM CS-4 · p5–8

Prior, likelihood, posterior, evidence

\(P(H\mid E)=P(E\mid H)P(H)/P(E)\): prior \(P(H)\) is belief before evidence, likelihood \(P(E\mid H)\), posterior \(P(H\mid E)\) after evidence, and \(P(E)\) is the normalising evidence. Posteriors over exhaustive hypotheses sum to 1.

ISM CS-4 · p15

Bayesian learning & MAP

Prior knowledge is combined with observed data to get the final probability of each hypothesis; a new instance is classified by the most probable (MAP) hypothesis, \(\hat Y=\arg\max_{y_k}P(y_k)\prod_i P(x_i\mid y_k)\).

ISM CS-4 · p19,29

Naïve Bayes classifier

A generative classifier applying Bayes' rule under the "naïve" assumption that features are conditionally independent given the class, so \(P(X_1,\dots,X_n\mid Y)=\prod_j P(X_j\mid Y)\). Effective for spam filtering, sentiment analysis, document and medical-diagnosis classification.

ISM CS-4 · p24–29

Conditional independence assumption

\(X\) is conditionally independent of \(Y\) given \(Z\) if \(P(X\mid Y,Z)=P(X\mid Z)\). Naïve Bayes makes this hold across all features, drastically simplifying the joint likelihood (real features rarely are, hence "naïve").

ISM CS-4 · p27–28

Laplace smoothing

If a feature value never appears with a class, its conditional probability is 0 and zeros out the whole product. Adding a count of 1 to every cell (Laplace/add-one smoothing) prevents this and restores usable estimates.

ISM CS-4 · p34–35,44
CS-5 — Random Variables & Probability Distributions · source: S1-26_ISM_CS-5_PPT.pdf

Random variable

A function mapping each sample point to a real number. Discrete RVs take countable values (heads in two tosses); continuous RVs take any value in an interval (height, waiting time).

ISM CS-5 · p3–5

Discrete probability distribution

Assigns \(P(X=x)\) to each value with \(P(x)\ge 0\) and \(\sum P(x)=1\). Fully describes a discrete RV (e.g. daily health status categories).

ISM CS-5 · p6–7

Discrete uniform distribution

The simplest discrete distribution: all \(n\) values equally likely with \(f(x)=1/n\) (e.g. a fair die, \(f(x)=1/6\)).

ISM CS-5 · p8

Mathematical expectation

\(E[X]=\sum x\,f(x)\) is the long-run average value. A game is "fair" when the expected gain is zero — the criterion used to judge betting games.

ISM CS-5 · p10–11

Variance of a random variable

\(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2]-\mu^2\); its root is the SD. Measures the spread of a RV around its expected value.

ISM CS-5 · p12–14

Cumulative distribution function

\(F(x)=P(X\le x)\) accumulates probabilities; for a discrete RV it is a step function of running sums that reaches 1 at the largest value.

ISM CS-5 · p15–16

Continuous RV & probability density function

For a continuous RV, a single point has probability 0; probabilities are areas under the pdf. \(P(x_1<X<x_2)=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}f(x)\,dx\), with \(f(x)\ge 0\) and total area 1.

ISM CS-5 · p23,27

Joint probability distribution

Describes two RVs together: discrete joint pmf \(f(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y)\) (bivariate distribution) or a joint density for continuous RVs.

ISM CS-5 · p34–37

Marginal distribution

The distribution of one variable obtained by summing (or integrating) the joint over the other; reported in the table margins, e.g. \(p(x)=\sum_y p(x,y)\).

ISM CS-5 · p38

Conditional distribution

The distribution of one RV given a value of the other, \(f(y\mid x)=f(x,y)/f_X(x)\) — e.g. \(P(\text{Flu}\mid \text{Fever})\) from a joint table.

ISM CS-5 · p41–44

Independent random variables

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent iff the joint factorises: \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) for all \(x,y\). Then \(E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]\).

ISM CS-5 · p40,50

Coefficient of variation for comparison

CV = SD/mean expresses spread relative to the mean, letting you compare variability across variables on different scales (e.g. protein shows the least variation among milk-quality metrics).

Webinar 1 · s15–16

Modality of data

A data set can be unimodal, bimodal, trimodal or multimodal depending on how many values tie for the highest frequency; it may also have no mode if no value repeats.

Webinar 1 · s6

Redundant-backup reliability

For independent components, the system survives unless all fail; \(P(\text{saved})=1-\prod P(\text{each crashes})\). Independence lets failure probabilities multiply.

Webinar 1 · s19

"Exactly one" event probability

\(P(\text{exactly one of }A,B)=P(A)+P(B)-2P(A\cap B)\); combining the addition rule with the "at least one" information recovers the intersection first.

EC Exams — Distributions, Inference, Correlation & Forecasting · source: EC-2 & EC-3 instruction sheets

Binomial & Poisson approximation

Counts of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials follow a binomial distribution; when \(n\) is large and \(p\) small, the Poisson with \(\lambda=np\) closely approximates it (fewer computations, similar answer).

EC-2 · Q6

Normal distribution & z-score

The bell-shaped continuous distribution central to inference; standardising \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) converts any normal value to the standard normal so probabilities can be read from a z-table.

EC-3 · Q5,Q8

Hypothesis testing of means (z-test)

State \(H_0\) (no difference) and \(H_1\), compute a test statistic from the sample, and compare with the critical value at a significance level (e.g. 5%). Used to test whether two population means differ when population SDs are known.

EC-3 · Q5

ANOVA (F-test)

Compares three or more population means simultaneously by contrasting between-group variance with within-group variance; a large F rejects the hypothesis of equal means.

EC-3 · Q6

Spearman's rank correlation

Measures monotonic association between two ranked variables (e.g. two judges' rankings); \(r_s\) near +1/−1 indicates strong agreement/disagreement, near 0 none.

EC-3 · Q4

Exponential smoothing (forecasting)

A weighted forecast where each new forecast blends the latest actual and the previous forecast via a smoothing constant \(\alpha\); larger \(\alpha\) reacts faster to recent data.

EC-3 · Q7

Gaussian Mixture Model

Models a density as a weighted sum of Gaussian components, \(p(x)=\sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\) with mixing weights summing to 1; underlies clustering and the EM algorithm.

EC-3 · Q8

Data preprocessing from summary statistics

A descriptive summary (count/mean/SD/quartiles/min/max) reveals outliers (via IQR), low-variation features (via SD or CV), and scaling needs — guiding preprocessing before ML.

Law of large numbers

The empirical (relative-frequency) approach rests on this law: over a large number of trials the empirical probability of an event approaches its true probability. The more observations you collect, the more accurate the probability estimate becomes — which is why frequencies from big data sets are trusted as probabilities.

ISM CS-2 · p12 (image)

Expectation & variance under linear transforms

Expectation is linear: scaling and shifting pass through, so \(E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b\). Variance ignores shifts but scales by the square, \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\); adding a constant never changes spread. \(E(XY)=E(X)E(Y)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)+b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) hold only when \(X,Y\) are independent. Note \(E(X/Y)\neq E(X)/E(Y)\) in general.
